Last Dinner: The Feast Goes On, but the Market Has Turned Picky

The earnings rout of late October is not liquidity draining away but a picky market: any growth downshift costs 20% or more in a day. What supports the "last dinner" call is a hiking Fed, a wave of new stock supply and the downshift penalty. A low around mid-November is most likely, with the December 9 Fed meeting setting the direction.

Naughty Boss . Empire Integration LLC · Independent research, not investment advice

中文版

In the week of October 26 to 29, America's large tech companies reported earnings and raised money in a cluster. Six of ten fell more than 10% (three of them in the October 30 pre-market), yet the S&P 500 fell only 0.3% on October 29. A popular reading says this is the last dinner of the artificial intelligence (AI) bull market: the market has adopted a new reward rule, money is moving from the "capital spending" story to a "trust layer", and Cloudflare is the stock of the new game. This note tests each of those claims against closing prices and the companies' own filings, then lays out the path week by week, from now to the end of March 2027, using the calendar.

Conclusion: the feast goes on, but the rules for what gets served have changed

The "last dinner" call partly holds. Three things support it, and they are not the three the popular reading offers: the Federal Reserve is raising rates, new listings and secondary offerings are draining money, and the market has started to punish growth downshifts hard. The popular reading rests on three beams: a "who pays" reward rule fitted to ten earnings reactions, a shift "from a liquidity-driven rally to a divergent market", and the "state rotating money between pipes". None survives a check against closing prices.

This is not liquidity draining away; it is a market that has turned picky. The rally ran on after the Fed's hike on September 16, and kept going while the 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.31% on October 5. Cloudflare set its high for the year in those very days. The engine was earnings and capital spending, not easy money. This week's clearest signal is a single one: when growth downshifts, by any amount, the market prices the company as having lost its growth identity. Five companies lost 20% to 30% in a day.

The "who pays" rule was fitted to the first after-hours quotes. Microsoft, the model "perfect score", rose as much as about 7% after hours, then closed down 0.2% the next day. Meta, described as falling 7%, actually closed up 2.0%. Oracle rose 10.7% and gave back more than half of it within two days. On closing prices, the rule falls apart.

Cloudflare is a strong candidate for the "verification layer", and also the company with the least room for error under the downshift rule. Its market value is about 44 times annual sales; ServiceNow's is about 7.4 times. For its report after the close on November 5, options price a move of about plus or minus 11%, almost the same as the 10.1% average actual move over its last eight reports, so the market is not leaning either way. In May it beat on revenue and still fell 23.6%, on a slightly soft next-quarter outlook and a record-low gross margin.

The most likely path is a low around mid-November, with the December 9 Fed meeting setting the direction. The season favors bulls: in the nineteen midterm elections since 1950, the S&P 500 rose from election day to the following March 31 eighteen times. Rates favor bears: 2018, the year most like today (Fed hikes, long rates at new highs, tech stocks crushed on earnings), fell further around the December Fed meeting and recovered only after the Fed turned. We put the main path at about 45%, a 2018-style path at about 35%, and a V-shaped recovery of the highs before the end of November at about 20%.

The longer "trust layer" thesis has the right direction but needs softer wording. Open source plus AI is making code itself cheaper; that is right. But cheapened code has historically fed companies like Red Hat and Databricks, because value flows to whoever takes responsibility for outcomes and owns the customer and the distribution. Standing in front of websites does not by itself confer pricing power: Akamai held the network edge for twenty years and became a low-growth utility. "Any feature's value goes to zero" should read "code gets cheaper; responsibility and distribution get dearer".

2. What the market punishes, and what it rewards

The popular reading fits the ten reactions to a formula: the reaction equals a weighted sum of "does capital spending have an end", "who pays" (own cash best, shareholder dilution worst) and "core business surprise", minus "growth downshift". We take each term in turn.

Growth downshifts get punished hard: holds. ServiceNow, Fortinet, Twilio, Reddit and Intel each fell 20% to 27% in a day. Fortinet makes the case best: it raised its full-year revenue guide and trimmed its service revenue guide by only about 1%, and still lost a fifth of its value. The market is not punishing a few points of growth; it is repricing whether the company is still a growth stock. But this is an old pattern, not evidence of a new game: Netflix fell 35.1% in one day in April 2022, and Meta 24.6% that October, the same kind of penalty.

"Who pays" decides reward and punishment: does not hold on closing prices; downgraded to doubtful. The popular reading's numbers look neat: Microsoft, expanding with its own cash, up 4%; Meta, making shareholders pay, down 7%. After checking: Microsoft rose as much as about 7% after hours and closed down 0.2% the next day; Meta's -7% appears nowhere, neither after hours on October 28 nor at any point on October 29, when it closed up 2.0%; Oracle gave back more than half of its gain within two days. Ten data points fitted with four weights and a yes-or-no term is about two points per parameter, too few to tell positioning, valuation and capital spending apart. We keep it as a test: measured from the October 29 close, if Microsoft outperforms the average of Alphabet and Amazon by 5 percentage points or more by November 30, it is upgraded to holds; if the reverse, it does not hold.

The absolute size of capital spending is not in the rule: cannot be tested. Oracle's huge capital spending did not stop its rally, but it gave back more than half within two days; one data point proves nothing.

3. Has the game changed?

"From a liquidity-driven rally to a divergent market": does not hold. Easy money did not drive this rally. Since the end of March, the Fed raised rates by 25 basis points to 3.75%-4.00% on September 16 by a 12-0 vote and held on October 27-28; the 3-month Treasury bill yield rose from 3.59% to 3.98%, and the 10-year from 4.35% to 5.31% on October 5, staying above 5.2% for twelve straight trading days from September 28 to October 13. Divergence did not start now either: the average 20-day correlation among the eight largest tech companies was only 0.12 in September, so divergence peaked during the rally. It is now 0.38 (76th percentile of the past year): the giants have started falling together, not going their separate ways.

"Giants crash, index calm" does not point in any direction. There have been six such days since 2018: Meta in July 2018, February 2022 and October 2022, Netflix in April 2022, Alphabet in October 2023 and Nvidia in January 2025, each a single stock down 10% to 35% in a day while the index held. In the 120 trading days that followed, the S&P 500 rose three times and fell three times. A single stock crashing while the index stays put is ordinary in earnings season, in bull and bear markets alike.

"The state is rotating money between pipes": does not hold. The Fed is tightening, and there is no sign of policy propping up the old pipes or lifting new ones. In 2022, when stocks and bonds fell together, and in 2000-2002, when the Nasdaq lost 78%, the authorities did not hold the line either. The same readings have a plainer explanation: the supply of new stock. After SpaceX raised $75 billion in June, the Nasdaq-100 fell 8.2% within 45 days; now Meta's roughly $97.5 billion raise and Anthropic's listing are taking the baton. Supply absorbs money; no state intent is required.

What does support the "last dinner" are these three things. The Fed is hiking; new listings and secondary offerings are draining money; companies whose growth downshifts lose more than a fifth of their value in a day. But credit is calm: the price ratio of high-yield bonds to Treasuries is still in the upper part of its one-year range, so this is not yet a bear market. To upgrade the title from doubtful to holds, we would need that ratio to break below its one-year low. If the giants' correlation rises above 0.5 and the VIX above 25, that is broad de-risking, not a picky market.

4. Where is the value moving?

Open source plus AI is cheapening code: the direction holds, "to zero" does not. Open source is the public knowledge base of human software engineering; AI trained on it cuts the cost of assembly from months to days, so writing code really is losing value. But cheapened code fed Red Hat (sold to IBM for $34 billion, completed in 2019) and Databricks; money flows to whoever is responsible for results. Software sold by the seat is still growing revenue by 10% to 20%, and the IGV software fund has risen 40% from its March 27 low; value migrates over years, not quarters. Value is moving toward four things: the capacity to take responsibility for outcomes, customer relationships and distribution, physical networks and computing power, and identity and verification.

AI agents make verification a necessity: demand holds, who collects is doubtful. When the marginal cost of a request approaches zero and identity is free, abuse is inevitable. Cloudflare disclosed on October 20 that this year, for the first time, more than half of internet traffic did not come from humans, and that daily requests from AI agents rose more than 1,700% in a year. But where the money gets collected is still open: the network standing in front of websites, the agent platforms themselves and the payment networks are all competing. Trust services can also be turned into free goods by open standards: since 2015 Let's Encrypt has made website certificates free, and the value of spam filtering ended up with the inbox platforms, not the networks in between.

Content owners are starting to close their doors; that favors the "border", but the evidence is early. In September Amazon blocked Meta's shopping agent Muse; Reddit now requires a login to read its old-style site (the old site only, not the whole site). The chain runs: content owners close their doors, which forces the big indexes to pay; payment requires metering; metering happens at the website's door. Every link makes sense, but none has a number yet.

Zero-sum is about budgets, not market value. Market value is not a fixed pot; one stock rising does not require another to fall. What is fixed is the customer's budget: from the same security or software budget, what one vendor gains another loses. Price-comparison sites and booking platforms did not bleed seller platforms dry over twenty years; they grew new toll collectors, so "buyer-side agents will bleed seller platforms" does not hold.

5. Cloudflare: a candidate, not a verdict

Why it is a candidate. Cloudflare's network spans 348 cities and interconnects directly with more than 13,000 networks; by W3Techs' count, about 24% of websites use it (not the one-third of popular accounts). On October 20 it disclosed that its developer base grew from 7.4 million to more than 10 million, up about 35% in three months. Developers sign up for free, and by our estimate it takes two to four quarters for sign-ups to become usage revenue, so these readings map to 2027 revenue. Its advantages are real: the network and its market share are hard to copy, and developers bring workloads, which bring threat data, so the wheel spins faster with each turn. But verification standards are open, and Akamai's history shows that an edge network alone cannot hold pricing power, so we rate it a "strong candidate", with time on the neutral-to-favorable side.

Why it also has the least room for error. Its market value is about 44 times annual sales. Under this week's downshift rule, the higher the multiple, the harsher the penalty for a step down. In May it beat revenue estimates by about 3% and still fell 23.6% the next day, only because its next-quarter outlook was slightly soft and its gross margin, at 72.8%, hit a record low. The popular claim that "it buys no chips and has no payer problem" is also inaccurate: it has deployed graphics processors at the edge for AI inference, and AI workloads are squeezing its margin.

What to watch in the November 5 report. Beating revenue estimates is close to routine; the stock will turn on three things: the next-quarter revenue guide against consensus, whether gross margin stops falling, and whether developer growth is starting to turn into paid usage. Options price a move of about plus or minus 11%, nearly the same as the 10.1% average actual move over the last eight reports, so there is no bargain in either direction, and the downside tail is fatter: the largest of the last eight moves was -23.6%. The next-day closes after the last eight reports were -4.6%, +17.8%, +6.5%, -3.6%, +13.8%, +5.2%, -23.6% and +5.6%: five up and three down, not the "six of seven up" of popular accounts.

Zero trust: a share challenger; the replacement thesis is doubtful. In zero trust (never trust any access by default; verify every time), the leader today is Zscaler, rated a Leader by Gartner. Cloudflare is cheaper and growing faster, and in June it released tooling to migrate customers from Zscaler and Palo Alto. But the market is not buying the replacement story for now: Zscaler rose 26% from October 16 to 26 and has nearly doubled from its April low.

Pay-per-request is an optional upside, not a valuation floor. The web protocol reserved a "payment required" status code (HTTP 402) back in 1997, and later pay-per-use schemes such as Flattr and Blendle failed on demand, not on technology. Today card networks, Stripe, Google and OpenAI are all building agent payments, and the card networks and Stripe also hold clearing licenses. "A clearing house going from hundreds of billions to trillions" does not hold at this stage; we will reassess when the company first discloses settlement volume on its own.

The price pattern: half holds. The three rallies this year each added about $100, which holds: $91 in May-June, $120 in June-August and $101 in September-October. But the percentage gains of 49%, 56% and 37.5% are not shrinking, and none of the three was started by an earnings gap. In its own history since 2019, the chance of rising another 33% or more within 32 trading days from any given day is only 16% to 20%, not one-half. So "410 by December" does not hold; if the report passes, the prior high near 370 is the first gate. Two checkpoints: a November 6 close below 285 (the October 20 low) means the retest of the low has failed; a close above 330 confirms the upward path.

Joining the S&P 500 is still far off. The rule requires the sum of the last four quarters of net income under generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) to be positive, and the latest quarter to be positive too. Cloudflare lost $170 million under GAAP in the second quarter (including $151 million of restructuring charges), so the earliest regular window is September 2027, and that is conditional.

6. The giants and the hardware chain

Microsoft: the "perfect score" earned no reward. Azure grew 48% and capital spending doubled; the stock rose as much as about 7% after hours and closed slightly lower the next day. A "perfect score" did not shield it from the giants being repriced together, so the rule is at least no good for picking stocks.

Alphabet and Amazon: the buyers get punished. Alphabet spent $51.4 billion in the quarter and its free cash flow turned negative; AWS grew 39%, its fastest in nearly five years, yet Amazon fell 7.7% pre-market, with 2026 capital spending of about $220 billion showing no sign of slowing. What the two share: capital spending with no end in sight, eating into cash flow.

Meta: an offering is not a top, but watch the supply. Of the roughly $97.5 billion raise, about $60 billion is an at-the-market program that will feed into the market over the coming months. The stock closed up 2.0%, so "the offering marks the top" has no price support for now; how fast that program is absorbed is the supply pressure of the months ahead.

The hardware chain: "a peak in the first half of 2027" is doubtful. Part of the chain has already moved: Micron is 23% below its June 25 high and Nvidia 10.7% below its October 6 high. The popular case rests on "three samples from different sources": Alphabet and Amazon punished as buyers, and Intel's falling guide. But the first two are the same mechanism, and Intel's problem is its own foundry business, so there are really only two. Historically, as with Cisco in 2000 and Micron in 2018, share prices peaked one to three quarters before revenue did; a share-price peak in the first half of 2027 would need evidence of a revenue peak in the second half of 2027 or in 2028, and there is none yet. OpenAI is still negotiating funding at a higher valuation (about $1.2-1.4 trillion in secondhand reports, above $852 billion in March). The real test comes in January and February: the combined 2027 capital spending growth that the four cloud giants guide to.

7. Software sold by the seat

The first stage, multiple compression, has happened; the second, "revenue itself falling", has not. The IGV software fund fell 24% in the first quarter of 2026: that was multiple compression. ServiceNow fell 27% this week, but the seat-based software companies seen as most at risk are still growing revenue at 13.7% to 20.5%, so "revenue has started to fall" does not hold. Also punished this week were network security hardware (Fortinet), advertising (Reddit) and chips (Intel): the penalty targets growth downshifts, not the seat model as such. This upgrades to holds only when the first large seat-based software company reports a year-over-year revenue decline; if ServiceNow's revenue growth is at least 19% in late January, the second stage is postponed by at least a quarter.

8. The calendar and the most likely path

Base rates first. In the nineteen midterm elections since 1950, the S&P 500 rose an average of 2.6% from election day to year-end (up thirteen times) and 10.0% to the following March 31 (up eighteen times), and was higher a year later all nineteen times; the only year still lower in March was the 2002 bear market. In 2018 it fell 9.0% to year-end and was up 2.9% by the next March. Since 2000, the November options-expiration week has averaged -0.27%, the December expiration week +0.05% with the week after at +0.69% (up 69% of the time), and the January expiration week -0.50% (up 42% of the time). After the last eight Nasdaq-100 peaks, the maximum drawdown within 60 trading days ranged from -13.6% to -35.7%, with the low between the 20th and 58th trading day; after true peaks (2000, 2007, 2021) the rebound recovered only 28% to 62% of the decline, after false peaks (2015, 2020, 2024, 2025) 96% to 114%. The Nasdaq-100 peaked on October 6; October 30 is the 18th trading day after, with a pre-market drawdown of 8.2%. On base rates, the low falls between November 3 and December 28.

Three paths (estimates). Main path, about 45%: after the earnings-season shakeout, midterm seasonality takes over, with a low around mid-November and a recovery after the December Fed meeting. 2018-style, about 35%: a hawkish Fed meeting and new highs in long rates, falling into year-end until the Fed turns. V-shaped, about 20%: the highs recovered before the end of November. The table follows the main path; the last column lists the signals that would switch it. On Anthropic's dates, only one thing is confirmed. On October 26 it publicly filed its registration statement (S-1) to list on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT, with the share count and price range not yet set. Under the Securities and Exchange Commission's rules since 2017, a company that filed confidentially may begin its roadshow no sooner than 15 days after the public filing, so November 10 at the earliest; the listing "before the midterms" that Reuters reported in September is therefore no longer possible. Reports put the target before Thanksgiving, which the company has not confirmed, so we treat it as a window, not a date.
