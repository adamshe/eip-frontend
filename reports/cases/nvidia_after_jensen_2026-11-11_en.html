Conclusion

This report's reading is this: the extra market value and gross margin NVIDIA has gained in the past few years were not earned by technology alone. They come from a demand machine built and run by one man, Jensen Huang: back challengers to take a share of the four big cloud companies' compute business and force an arms race, close the loop with circular financing, sell urgency at three levels (companies, nations and individuals), and bring about demand beyond what is actually needed and a false shortage (the rise in memory prices included). Nobody else can run this machine, so when he goes, the premium goes with him.

The machine has one more link upstream: TSMC and the Taiwan supply chain, out of decades of trust in him personally, are willing to expand before orders are firm, and that is what lets him promise customers delivery dates and volumes ahead of anyone else. Demand is made downstream, trust is held upstream, and both ends sit in the same pair of hands: this is master-level orchestration, and it is also why the machine cannot be handed over.

First, the status of the evidence, stated once. The facts along this chain (equity stakes, guarantees, sovereign orders, gross margin, memory price increases, supply commitments) each have a source; the intent and the causation are this report's inference, not established fact. Three further points have no report that names them and are likewise inference: that Jensen Huang personally sets the allocations, that buyers over-ordered to secure a place in line, and that suppliers expand before orders are firm. The seven links below are stated plainly, without a qualifier on every sentence; which parts of each link are fact and which are inference is set out in the evidence table further down; the evidence on the other side is gathered in the "Counter-evidence" section. The weakest is the fourth link, because compute rents are rising and the cloud companies all say they lack capacity. On "does the premium really recede once he goes", this piece rates the evidence as questionable: not yet confirmed, and not yet falsified.

The machine has seven links. The first four turn in time order, the fifth is the machine's product, and the sixth says why it cannot be transferred; the seventh sits upstream and is the precondition for the links before it.

First link: backing challengers. In 2023 NVIDIA took a stake in CoreWeave, then still very small, and let it get chips first; later it also joined a funding round for Nebius. The new clouds had chips and the big cloud companies did not have enough, so Microsoft turned around and rented compute from the new clouds, and OpenAI gave CoreWeave contracts worth well over ten billion dollars. Compute had been the four big cloud companies' business; now a piece of it had been carved off. What the four saw was this: if they did not scramble for chips, their own tenants would be drawn away. That fear lit the arms race: the stakes came first and the spending increases after, with the four companies' capital spending at about 150 billion dollars combined in 2023 and guided to about 720-740 billion dollars combined for 2026.

Second link: circular financing closes the loop. NVIDIA invests in or guarantees a customer; the customer uses that credit to buy chips, often then pledges the chips as collateral and borrows more to buy more chips; the chips sold are booked as NVIDIA revenue as usual, the stock rises, and there is capital for the next round of investment. By July 2026 NVIDIA carried 51.2 billion dollars of non-marketable equity investments, against 22.3 billion half a year earlier; it provides credit support of up to 105 billion dollars for the data centers of an OpenAI-related party, put another 2 billion dollars into CoreWeave in January 2026, and has committed to buy the compute CoreWeave cannot sell.

Third link: manufactured urgency, sold one layer at a time. To companies the message is "sold out", and allocation became a matter of personal favor: Oracle's founder has said himself that he and Elon Musk "begged" Jensen Huang for chips over dinner. To nations the message is "sovereign AI", every country needing compute of its own; that revenue exceeded 30 billion dollars last fiscal year. To individuals the message is an AI supercomputer on every desk and the age of robots. Each layer was rolled out as the one before it neared saturation.

Fourth link: demand was pulled forward, beyond what can actually be put to use. Chips arrive with no power to plug them into, and buyers keep buying; the scramble spilled over into memory, where contract prices for conventional memory (DRAM) rose more than 90% in a single quarter, the first quarter of 2026, and were still rising in the fourth. This is a false shortage: there really are not enough chips to go around, but the part that is missing comes from the scramble, not from use.

Fifth link: the evidence is in the gross margin. The CUDA software ecosystem has existed since 2007 and has been there all along; gross margin before the arms race was 64.9% in fiscal 2022 and 56.9% in fiscal 2023, and after it 72.7% in fiscal 2024 and 75.0% in fiscal 2025. The extra ten-odd percentage points are a scarcity premium: a product of this machine, not of the technology.

Sixth link: the machine is not transferable. In scarcity, who gets chips first is his personal call; the story is told by him on stage; the deals with heads of state and with customers' founders are negotiated by him. A successor can inherit the products and the software ecosystem, but allocation would have to run on contracts and queues; it would stop being a personal favor, and the scarcity premium would lose its source.

The seventh link sits upstream: trust on the supply side. Chips have to be made before anyone can decide who gets them. TSMC's leading-edge process and advanced packaging, and Taiwan's server contract-manufacturing chain, are willing to expand for him before orders are firm, and what that rests on is decades of personal ties: TSMC's founder wrote in his memoir that in 2013 he asked him to take over as TSMC's chief executive; in 2025 and 2026 he hosted dinners in Taipei for the heads of the supply-chain companies, as local media reported. Because suppliers are willing to share the risk, he can vouch to customers for delivery dates and volumes ahead of anyone else, and that assurance is worth money in itself. Supply has to be secured first for there to be any power to allocate, and only then can the first four links turn. Contracts and reserved capacity stay when the person changes; the trust behind expanding first and coming to the rescue first cannot be taken along and cannot be passed on.

The machine also has a reverse side: the chips and the debt are left in other people's hands. The lenders to the new clouds hold collateral that loses value every year; cloud company shareholders bear the depreciation; sovereign buyers are spending taxpayers' money; and the rise in memory prices is paid by people buying computers and phones. NVIDIA is inside the loop too: 279 billion dollars of supply commitments and the guarantees above are a moat while demand is strong and a fixed burden the moment the machine stops.

This report also carries a long-run estimate with no date attached: about 80 dollars per share, a market value of about 2 trillion dollars. At that level NVIDIA is an ordinary, mature company earning what it ought to earn, with no guru premium left. The "extra market value" is measured against that figure: it follows from this long-run estimate (an estimate, not a measurement) that about half of the roughly 4.2 trillion dollar market value at the November 11 close is this machine's premium. The figure is below the price zone of every path in "The paths from here" further down, because those zones cover only the repricing of the key person in the weeks to months after a board decision, while 80 dollars describes where things land once the premium has receded entirely. The arithmetic and its premises are in the section "The long-run estimate"; it is this report's estimate, not a forecast and not an instruction to buy or sell.

How this reading gets decided. It has a direct rival: the demand is real and Jensen Huang merely organized the supply; in that case the premium does not recede after a change at the top. The rival's evidence is real, not decoration: advanced packaging capacity is genuinely tight, the cloud companies' order backlogs are growing, and one-year H100 contract rents went from 1.70 dollars per chip-hour in October 2025 back up to 2.10-2.70 dollars in spring 2026 (all of it is laid out in the "Counter-evidence" section). Today's prices cannot tell the two cases apart, because chips sell out in both; what can tell them apart is gross margin guidance, delivery lead times, order cancellations and rents on older models once he is no longer leading. The first reading is the third-quarter report on November 17, then the annual report in late February 2027 and the developer conference in March.

The first link needs to be stated precisely. The new clouds' largest customers are in fact the big cloud companies and OpenAI: Microsoft rents compute from CoreWeave and Nebius, and OpenAI has given part of its contracts to CoreWeave. So the mechanism is not that the new clouds took scattered customers away from the big cloud companies; it is that new clouds holding chips first left the big cloud companies no choice but to rent from them, and gave the big cloud companies' largest tenants somewhere else to go. For the big cloud companies this matters more than losing a few customers: they discovered that where the chips go is not theirs to decide.

The collateral in the second link loses value. NVIDIA brings out a new generation every year, and the rents and residual values of older chips fall with each one; the loans secured by chips run for years (CoreWeave's March 2026 loan matures in 2032 [38]). This is the most fragile point in the loop: the chips' value is held up by new demand, and part of that new demand is held up by these loans.

Counter-evidence: the shortage may also be real

The seven-link reading has a direct rival: the demand is real, supply is held back by physical limits, and Jensen Huang has simply organized supply better than anyone else. There is a good deal of evidence for the rival, and all of it is set out here.

The supply side has physical constraints. Advanced packaging (TSMC's CoWoS) is the bottleneck for these chips: estimates of monthly capacity at the end of 2026 range from 88,000 to 130,000 wafers, with NVIDIA taking about 60% (Morgan Stanley's estimate, as relayed secondhand [60]). A packaging line cannot be talked into existence.

The buyers themselves say there is not enough. Amazon said in August 2026 that it still lacked capacity that year to meet all demand [11]; its cloud business grew about 39% in the third quarter, with an order backlog of 578 billion dollars (secondhand); Meta set 2026 capital spending at 135-145 billion dollars [12]. These companies are spending their own cash flow, with boards and shareholders watching.

Prices are on this side. One-year H100 contract rents have risen roughly 25% to 60% from the October 2025 low [10]. If a large amount of frontier compute were idle, rents should be falling. So "chips gathering dust" does not hold as a general phenomenon; what holds is two local kinds of idleness, hardware delivered but not yet powered and low utilization in enterprises' own clusters.

The spark may not have been him. ChatGPT launched at the end of November 2022, before NVIDIA took stakes in the new clouds; the cloud companies' fear of one another, and of OpenAI, was enough on its own to set off a race. The four companies' capital spending totaled about 150 billion dollars in 2023, about 245 billion in 2024 and about 410 billion in 2025 (secondhand tally, definitions vary) [56], and the climb began before the new clouds amounted to much.

Gross margin has other explanations. The high-margin data center business has grown sharply as a share of revenue in these years (data center revenue of 193.7 billion dollars in fiscal 2026, about 90% of the year's 215.9 billion, secondhand; the shares in earlier years were not checked for this piece); the fiscal 2023 low may include the effect of inventory charges (the specific figure was not checked for this piece); the 71.1% for fiscal 2026 includes a 4.5 billion dollar H20 charge in the first quarter (non-GAAP gross margin that quarter was 61.0%, or 71.3% excluding the charge) [5]. Treating 64.9% and 56.9% as "the technology's gross margin" is a simplification.

NVIDIA itself does not accept the term "circular financing". Reportedly, the company said in a November 2025 memo to sell-side analysts that it does not rely on providing financing to customers to grow revenue; the chief financial officer said on the August 2026 call that some people call these arrangements circular financing and the company does not see them that way (both as relayed, not verbatim) [42].

The rise in memory prices also has another cause: memory makers have moved capacity to the high-bandwidth memory used in AI servers, so the supply of ordinary memory has shrunk. That is a capacity choice and need not be a scramble.

Supply-side trust also has an institutional side. What gets NVIDIA its capacity is first of all volume and money: it is reported to be TSMC's largest customer (secondhand; the share figures go no further than a search summary, and the annual report itself was not checked), its supply commitments are 279 billion dollars, and its annual report states that it has prepaid manufacturing and capacity agreements [6][7][59]. These are written into contracts, belong to the company, and stay when the chief executive changes; to a supplier, the largest customer is the largest customer whoever runs it. Only two things belong to the person: expanding before orders are firm, and helping him first when something goes wrong; there is no public data on how large these two are, and this piece found no report naming a supplier that expanded ahead of orders. The test is to watch what happens after a change at the top: whether advanced packaging capacity tilts toward custom-chip customers, and whether NVIDIA's delivery lead times lengthen relative to others'. If neither happens, the personal part of the seventh link is not large.

Precedent: Cisco, Lucent and Nortel in 2000

The last time sellers on a large scale used their own credit to underwrite their buyers' demand was the telecom equipment industry in 2000. At the end of September 2000 Lucent had credit commitments to customers of up to about 6.7 billion dollars, of which about 1.3 billion had been drawn, and separately guaranteed about 1.4 billion dollars of customer debt (annual report notes, cited secondhand) [50]. Nortel had undrawn customer financing commitments of about 4.1 billion dollars at the end of 2000, against 2.4 billion a year earlier [51]. In the quarter it reported on May 8, 2001, Cisco took a 2.25 billion dollar charge for excess inventory; revenue that quarter was 4.73 billion dollars, below the 4.93 billion of the same quarter a year earlier [49]; the public explanation at the time was that customers had placed duplicate orders with several vendors to secure supply, and that once lead times shortened, orders were cancelled in clusters.

What is the same is the mechanism: the seller's balance sheet props up the buyer's purchasing power, and part of the orders placed in a shortage are there to hold a place in line. What is different must be stated too: NVIDIA's arrangements are mainly equity investments, guarantees and backstop purchase commitments, not direct lending; its large buyers are cash-rich cloud companies, not new telecom carriers living on bond issues; and the company itself says it does not depend on financing customers to grow [42]. One more point cuts neither for nor against this report's reading: Cisco in 2000 came to a halt while its chief executive was in office. A machine like this does not have to wait for the person to leave before it stops; with him gone, there is simply one less person who could keep it turning a while longer.

What has happened, and how much the market has priced in

His status has moved from a tail risk to the main scenario, but it is not yet settled. On November 8 NVIDIA's board issued a statement: because of reports that founder and chief executive Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, the board opened an independent review, overseen by a committee of independent directors with the help of outside counsel; the statement also said there is no reason to believe national security or company confidential information was compromised, and that the board has confidence in him and the management team. After the close on November 11, reported by the Wall Street Journal, the board is preparing to have him step down (not confirmed by the company; as of this update no 8-K, the filing for material events, had appeared); the evening's sequence of events, the sources and the estimates for each path are in the section "The paths from here". This company, worth about 4.2 trillion dollars at the close, has had the same chief executive for 33 years, has no chief operating officer, has about 60 people reporting directly to him, and names no successor in its proxy statement. The worry that "without him it falls apart" has a basis in the organizational structure.

The readings on the business itself have not loosened today. Gross margin in the latest quarter (ended July 2026) was 75.0%, with 74.0% guided for the next; one-year H100 contract rents went from a low of 1.70 dollars per chip-hour in October 2025 back to 2.10-2.70 dollars in spring 2026; Amazon says this year's capacity is still not enough to meet all demand. The idleness that does exist is two local phenomena: chips that arrived with no power to plug into, and low utilization in enterprises' own clusters. One reading is a timing gap, with power and buildings failing to keep up; this report reads it as buying faster than using. Today's prices cannot separate the two readings.

Now the next two to three years. Buyer power is rising, but that does not need him to leave: Broadcom's quarterly AI chip revenue is already about one fifth of NVIDIA's data center revenue, Google is supplying up to one million TPUs to Anthropic, and the research firm Omdia expects custom chips to outnumber GPUs by unit count in 2028. All of this happened while he was in office and at his most persuasive on stage. Seen from this side alone, he is an amplifier, not the accelerator pedal. This report's reading is the opposite: the cloud companies' fear of falling behind was lit precisely by the new clouds that were backed; and buyers turning to chips of their own design shows precisely that they want to get out of this machine.

On the evidence, the link that depends most on him personally is Washington's trust and head-of-state sovereign sales. Sovereign AI revenue last fiscal year was more than 30 billion dollars, about 14% of that year's 215.9 billion dollars of revenue; export licenses, travel with the President and deals negotiated directly with heads of state all depend heavily on him personally. The cause of the November 8 review falls squarely on this link.

So the reading is: the market has already poured out a good part of this half glass. The stock closed at 173.73 dollars on November 11 and traded at about 158.8 dollars after hours, about one third below the October 6 record close of 239.24 dollars. Of that decline, about 65% came before the November 8 statement (the stock was already down 22% at the November 6 close) and about 35% came in the three trading days after the statement plus the after-hours session on the 11th. This piece did not establish the cause of the October decline and does not assign it to either side. At first publication the forward price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) was about 14.6, below the roughly 19-20 of the S&P 500; on the same expected earnings the after-hours price works out to about 13 (this piece's own conversion), so the market is already discounting "how long these profits can last". On this report's reading, if he really leaves, what loosens first is personal discretion over allocation, personal trust on the supply side and the slightly more than one tenth of revenue tied to sovereign and policy channels, followed by the part of gross margin above its pre-arms-race level. Only gross margin below 70% for two straight quarters together with a sequential decline in data center revenue would count as this reading coming true, and neither is in today's readings; the observations used to decide it are in the comparison table in the "Counter-evidence" section. The first checkpoint is the board's formal decision (a company announcement or an 8-K is what counts), the second is the third-quarter report on Tuesday, November 17.

Five historical comparisons show that with a change of chief executive, the outcome depends on buyer structure and succession preparation, not on the change itself (the moves in each case are this piece's own calculations from historical daily closing prices [48]). Intel announced on June 21, 2018 that its chief executive had resigned for violating the company's relationship policy: the stock fell 2.4% that day, was down 2.9% a month later and 14.8% six months later (the S&P 500 fell 9.4% over the same six months), and the company raised its quarterly guidance the same day (public record, not rechecked for this piece). McDonald's dismissed its chief executive for a similar reason on November 3, 2019 and named an internal successor the same day: the stock fell 2.7% the next day, was down 0.4% a month later and 7.6% six months later (S&P 500 down 6.5%, a period that includes the pandemic). Uber's founder resigned under investor pressure in June 2017 (public record, not rechecked for this piece), when the company was not yet listed; in January 2018 a SoftBank-led purchase of existing shares closed at a valuation of about 48 billion dollars, 30% below the prior round's roughly 68.5 billion [34]. Apple's founder resigned on August 24, 2011 and a successor rehearsed for years took over the same day: the stock fell 0.7% the next day, was up 7.5% a month later and 38.9% six months later (S&P 500 up 16.0% over the same six months). Microsoft changed chief executives on January 13, 2000: the stock rose 4.1% the next day, was down 26.8% six months later and 50.4% twelve months later (S&P 500 up 4.2% and down 9.0% over the same periods), and its P/E went from above 50 at the change to below 12 by 2010 (secondhand), a period that also included the antitrust case and the collapse of the internet bubble [35]. In the Intel and McDonald's cases the stock ended six months within 6 percentage points of the market and the business carried on. NVIDIA's largest difference from those two is that its buyers are a few large companies able to design their own chips and that it has no rehearsed successor; Uber (a 30% valuation discount) and Microsoft (a decade of valuation compression) are cases of a founder premium being repriced and are the closer comparisons. All five are references only; the sample is small and cannot be treated as a probability.

If, after a change of chief executive, gross margin guidance holds for two quarters and investments in customers continue to be signed, the business runs the same with a different person and the hypothesis that "the margin premium is a key-person product" is weakened. If any report is corrected or retracted by its outlet, the corresponding fact is downgraded and the probabilities roll back.

The long-run estimate: about 80 dollars

This section is to be read separately from the path zones above. The path zones describe repricing in the weeks to months after a board decision; this section describes a landing point with no date attached, which is not part of the weighted arithmetic of roughly 138-148 dollars above and does not correspond to any one path.

This report's long-run estimate is about 80 dollars per share (an estimate, not a measurement), a market value of about 2 trillion dollars (on about 24.1 billion shares, 80 x 24.1 billion is about 1.93 trillion). The meaning: after the machine stops, NVIDIA is still a good company, with products and a software ecosystem, but it no longer carries a guru premium and earns, in an ordinary way, the share it ought to earn.

The arithmetic is set out here, and every input is an estimate. First, gross margin returns to its floor from before the arms race, about 57-65% (the fiscal 2023 and fiscal 2022 levels), against 71.1% in fiscal 2026 and 75.0% in fiscal 2025 (GAAP). Second, the valuation multiple for growth compresses: a mature hardware company that is no longer growing fast, valued at about 12-14 times earnings (this multiple is an assumption taken by this piece in order to lay the arithmetic out, not the basis on which the 80 dollars was reached). Apply both to the earnings of the past twelve months: at first publication the stock was 176.7 dollars and the trailing P/E about 22.3, which implies earnings per share of about 7.9 dollars; with gross margin going from about 75% back to 57-65%, gross margin is 10-18 percentage points lower, which is 13% to 24% less gross profit; operating expenses do not fall with it and are estimated at 8-9% of revenue (this ratio is an estimate not checked against the financial statements; taken anywhere between 8% and 11%, earnings per share move by no more than about 0.1 dollars, a little over 1 dollar on the share price), so earnings per share are about 15% to 27% lower, leaving about 5.8-6.7 dollars; multiplied by 12-14, that is about 70-94 dollars, and 80 dollars lies within it. Of the two inputs that matter most, the gross margin floor comes from the company's own results over the years and the multiple is an assumption: at 10 times the result is about 58-67 dollars; at 16 times, about 93-107 dollars. So this arithmetic only shows what conditions 80 dollars requires; it does not validate it.

This arithmetic carries a heavier premise that needs to be stated on its own; it falls out of the arithmetic and is not an input that comes with the estimate itself. The premise is that earnings stay at the level of the past twelve months and stop growing. The market currently expects earnings per share of about 12.1 dollars over the next twelve months (176.7 divided by the forward P/E of 14.6), and 80 dollars is only about 6.6 times that. In other words, getting to 80 dollars takes not only a fall in gross margin but also the failure of the growth the market expects, that is, the demand pulled forward in the fourth link going back. Valued instead at the S&P 500's roughly 19-20 times, 80 dollars corresponds to earnings per share of only about 4 dollars, which would require revenue to fall a further 30% or so from its level of the past twelve months (estimate).

That is why 80 dollars is below the zone of every path above: the path zones reprice only the key-person discount and do not assume that growth fails; 80 dollars assumes the premium in all seven links recedes entirely. It is this report's estimate, has no date, and is neither a forecast nor an instruction to buy or sell. This piece's rating of the evidence on "does the premium recede entirely once he goes" remains questionable: if the "shortage is real" side in the "Counter-evidence" section holds, gross margin and growth both hold up and this price is not reached.

1. A test from the demand side: five links

From the side of demand and the buyers, the same thesis can be broken into five links and tested. This section through section 5 are that test; where it differs from the seven-link reading, the difference is stated in place. The five links are: he leaves; his personal persuasive power and authority are the engine behind the big cloud companies' arms race, Middle Eastern and other sovereign purchases, and the open ecosystem; without him buyers stop being persuaded; many of the chips already sold are sitting idle, so the true face of demand shows; pricing power moves from seller to buyer, and seller monopoly becomes buyer monopoly. Strung into a chain, the conclusion is at most as strong as its weakest link. Below, each link is checked for its mechanism, the opposing mechanism and the evidence.

2. How close the company sits to one person

Very close, by structure. Jensen Huang is 63, owns about 3.56% and is the largest individual shareholder, but has no super-voting shares; the company has no chief operating officer and no chief compliance officer, with compliance handled by a committee of the chief financial officer, the executive vice president of operations and the senior vice president of human resources [2]. The proxy statement names no successor; he reportedly said on a podcast in March 2026 that he does not believe in traditional succession planning, relies on continually passing knowledge to colleagues, and hopes the next chief executive comes from inside [3]. The possible interim choices mentioned in reports are lead independent director Stephen Neal and chief financial officer Colette Kress [2]. Compare Apple: when Steve Jobs resigned in 2011, Tim Cook had been chief operating officer for years and had run the company during Jobs's medical leaves. NVIDIA has no successor the market has seen rehearsed; this is the part of the whole reading that stands up best.

3. Five tests

1. Are the chips sitting idle?

For: Microsoft chief executive Satya Nadella said on a podcast in late 2025 that there may be a bunch of chips sitting in inventory that cannot be plugged in, with power and building shells as the bottleneck [8]; a company that sells cloud cost-optimization software found average GPU utilization of only 5% in the enterprise Kubernetes clusters it analyzed [9]; these are enterprise clusters, and the seller has its own interest. Against, and harder because it is price: one-year H100 contract rents have risen roughly 25% to 60% from the October 2025 low [10]; Amazon said in August 2026 that it still lacked capacity that year to meet all demand [11]; Meta set 2026 capital spending at 135-145 billion dollars [12]. If a large amount of frontier compute were idle, rents should fall, not rise. Verdict: the claim that frontier clusters are sitting idle is not supported; what exists is two local kinds of idleness, hardware delivered but not yet powered and low utilization in enterprise clusters. This link weakens the whole chain the most.

2. Did he talk the arms race into being?

By mechanism, each cloud company raises capital spending because it fears losing customers on AI platforms, and each points to its own orders in hand. The opposing mechanism: his roadmap launches and the "AI factory" framing convinced each of them that not following meant falling behind, which played a coordinating role. The evidence leans to the former: when Google and Amazon gave their direction for 2027, they talked about their own demand and capacity shortfalls (as relayed secondhand), not about NVIDIA's launch events. On that basis, his departure would weaken coordination, but the engine is not him. That tested only one channel, the "talking". The seven links point to another: backing new clouds, so that the big cloud companies have to rent chips from them and their largest tenants have somewhere else to go (see the first link). In time order ChatGPT's launch came first and the stakes in the new clouds after, so "the arms race was lit by him" remains an inference.

3. Sovereign orders and Washington: the link most dependent on the person

This is the link where the evidence best supports this report's reading. The partnership with Saudi Arabia's HUMAIN, 18,000 GB300 chips in a first phase and several hundred thousand over five years, was announced on May 13, 2025, during his visit to the Gulf with the President [17][2]; the first 200-megawatt phase of the United Arab Emirates data center was planned to be ready in the third quarter of 2026 [18]. The chief financial officer said sovereign AI revenue more than tripled last fiscal year to over 30 billion dollars [16], about 14% of that year's 215.9 billion dollars of revenue [5]. These deals are not only commercial negotiations; they are also export licensing and diplomatic arrangements, and the November 8 event touches exactly the point of "whether Washington continues to trust him". Gap: neither this fiscal year's sovereign revenue figure nor the actual 2026 go-live volume of each project is public, so the size of this link can only be estimated at somewhat over one tenth.

4. Allocation power and capacity reservations

In scarcity the seller decides who gets chips first, and that is one source of pricing power; one reading is that this power arrives with scarcity and leaves when supply catches up with demand, with little to do with who is chief executive. This report's reading is the opposite: part of the scarcity itself was organized by him, and personal discretion over allocation is precisely the source of the premium (the sixth link). The two readings are decided by delivery lead times and gross margin guidance after a change at the top. Capacity reservation rests on the balance sheet: as of July 26, 2026, supply commitments had risen from 119 billion dollars a quarter earlier to 279 billion, total future commitments were 366 billion, inventory had risen from 21.4 billion at the start of the year to 31.6 billion, and non-marketable equity investments were carried at 51.2 billion (22.3 billion at the start of the year), with a further 25 billion committed [6]. This part is written into contracts and does not leave with the person, but it cuts both ways: when demand slows, commitments and inventory are where the exposure shows first. The seventh link is about a layer outside the contracts: suppliers are willing to expand before orders are firm because of trust in him personally; how large that layer is, see the evidence table and the "Counter-evidence" section.

5. Roadmap and software ecosystem

The annual product cadence and the CUDA software ecosystem are organizational capabilities; the company said at its March 2026 developer conference that there are about 6 million CUDA developers (secondhand) [21], and the technical interface between the engineering teams and TSMC does not hang on one person either (the seventh link refers to a different layer, the personal trust that makes suppliers willing to expand first; see above). The opposing mechanism is that Google's JAX, OpenAI's Triton and PyTorch are decoupling model code from specific chips and lowering the cost of switching chips. That force was already advancing while he was in office; his departure would not change its direction, only possibly its speed.

4. Will seller monopoly turn into buyer monopoly?

Pricing power rests on three things: scarce supply, a lead in the roadmap, and a story that organizes buyers into a race. Seen from the demand side alone, without the key person the third loosens first; the first loosens with the capacity cycle, the second with the progress of the engineering teams and of rivals. On the seven-link reading, part of the first was also organized by him (the fourth and sixth links). The readings on buyers: as of July 26, five direct customers accounted for 22%, 14%, 13%, 11% and 10% of accounts receivable [6]; in the first half of this year three direct customers accounted for 16%, 15% and 13% of revenue (from a reprint of the 10-Q; that section of the original could not be read). Direct customers are mostly contract system builders; the real end buyers are a few cloud companies (the chief financial officer has said the five largest clouds account for over half of data center revenue, secondhand). These few buyers all have the ability to design their own chips, which is the precondition for buyer monopoly: Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue for the quarter ended August 2, 2026 was 16.7 billion dollars, with 21.7 billion guided for the next quarter, about one fifth of NVIDIA's 89 billion dollars of data center revenue in the same period (in dollars) [13]; Google is supplying up to one million TPUs to Anthropic [14]; Omdia expects 10.5 million custom chips against 10.1 million GPUs in 2028 (by unit count; GPUs cost far more each, so in dollars GPUs remain much larger, an inference) [15]. This precondition already exists, and it formed while he was in office. There are four observable signals: gross margin, prepayment and long-term contract terms, the share of buyers' capital spending going to in-house chips, and delivery lead times. Gross margin has not loosened today; public data on the other three is incomplete.

5. Three precedents

Apple is the counterexample, and it also shows the conditions under which the counterexample holds. On August 24, 2011, the day Steve Jobs resigned, Apple was worth about 349 billion dollars [23]; over the following decade and more it rose to several trillion. It held for two reasons: the buyers were hundreds of millions of dispersed consumers, none of whom could build an iPhone themselves; and Tim Cook was a successor rehearsed for years. NVIDIA's buyers are exactly the opposite, a few large companies able to design their own chips, and its successor has not been rehearsed.

Intel's market value peaked in August 2000 at about 500 billion dollars, by which time Andy Grove had stepped down as chief executive two years earlier and was still chairman; on August 2, 2024 it fell at one point to about 97 billion dollars [24]. The causes of the decline were missing the phone, repeated delays in manufacturing process, and one of its largest customers, Apple, moving its Macs to in-house chips from 2020. This shows that buyer self-design is a slow variable; once it starts, it happens without a founder having to leave.

Cisco is the most direct counterexample in structure. When its stock peaked on March 27, 2000 (80.06 dollars, split-adjusted), John Chambers was in office, and he stayed until 2015, yet the stock fell more than 80% over the following two-plus years (estimate) and only regained the peak with a close of 80.25 dollars on December 10, 2025 [22]. The cause was a break in the capital spending of telecom and internet companies, while Cisco at the time was also providing financing to customers. A key person in place cannot hold back a buyer cycle: demand propped up by the seller's credit does not have to wait for the person to leave before it stops (the figures for Cisco, Lucent and Nortel in those years are in the precedent section above). NVIDIA's investments in and credit support for customers today make this precedent more worth watching than Apple.

The price the market pays for each dollar of NVIDIA's expected earnings is already below that of the average S&P 500 company, which is a discount on "how long the profits can last".

Second, the third-quarter report on Tuesday, November 17, 2026: revenue against the 108 billion dollar guidance, gross margin against 74.0% (plus or minus 0.5 percentage points), and the fourth-quarter guidance and the changes in supply commitments and inventory; whether the call is held as usual, and who leads it. Gross margin within or above the guided range says pricing power has not loosened.

Third, Congress: whether the November 18 closed-door briefing takes place, and whether a subpoena is issued.

Fourth, the board's independent review: its findings and timing have not been announced. If export licenses are tightened or a sovereign project is halted, the sovereign link turns from a risk into a loss.

Fifth, the annual results season in late February 2027: whether this fiscal year's sovereign AI revenue is disclosed, and whether it is above 30 billion dollars.

Sixth, the cloud companies' capital spending guidance in January-February 2027, and whether the ratio of Broadcom's AI revenue to NVIDIA's data center revenue rises from about one fifth to more than three tenths.

Seventh, compute rents: if one-year H100 contracts fall below the October 2025 low of 1.70 dollars, the idle-chip claim has to be assessed again.
