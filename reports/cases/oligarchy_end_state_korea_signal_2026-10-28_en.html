The Oligarchy End-State, Read Through Korea: Ten Years of Non-Chaebol Stocks Paying Less Than Cash

A popular end-game thesis holds that oligarchy plus the state is capitalism's final form. Its clearest test is not in demographic statistics but in the Korean stock market. Over the ten years to October 2026, the median stock on Korea's main board outside the five big chaebol groups (the family-controlled conglomerates) returned 0.0% a year, while bank deposits paid 2.2%; six in ten of those stocks lagged cash and half of them lost money. Korea crossed the line where ordinary stocks stop paying more than cash in the ten-year window that ended in 2024. Measured the same way, the median US stock returned 7.8% a year over the same decade, which puts the US roughly where Korea stood in 2015, still some distance from that line.

Conclusion: the direction is right, the end point is early, the exits are too few, and opportunity is written off too soon

The thesis makes three claims; its direction holds up, but all three conclusions go too far. It says oligarchy plus the state is the final form; that the only exits are revolution or the state taking equity in the oligarchs and handing the money out; and that startups caught in between have no chance. Money really is concentrating, and the US labor share has slid for two decades. But "final", "two exits" and "no chance" all go too far.

Today, Korea's end-state signal is already lit in its stock market. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix earn about two-thirds of the profit of Korea's 125 largest listed companies. The rest miss the growth, and their share prices stall for years. The US resembles Korea only in tech: its five most profitable companies are all tech firms, but together they earn about 30% of large-company profit, against two firms earning two-thirds in Korea.

Fertility is a real ruler, but it measures the price of a ticket to family life, not corporate concentration itself. The total fertility rate (the average number of children a woman has over her lifetime) is 1.6 in the US, about where Korea was around 1990; Korea's rate last year was half that. Taiwan, an economy of small and mid-sized firms with no chaebol, came in at 0.7 last year, below Korea. Across 30 rich countries, the top income share and fertility are almost uncorrelated. Korean births have risen for two years, but mostly as first children of weddings the pandemic postponed: a catch-up, not a change of course.

Over the next few years the blade comes from the two frontier labs' valuation arithmetic, and it cuts share prices first. Anthropic and OpenAI, each valued near a trillion dollars, must pull revenue out of software and outsourcing to justify it. The predicted fall of incumbents "from 100 to 20" has already happened in share prices: call centers, stock images, translation, homework help and mid-sized outsourcers are down 60% to 97% since 2023. Yet most of their revenue has held; only homework help lost half. The labs' largest partners are old-line outsourcers such as Accenture and Deloitte; the cut lands on their mid-sized peers. Licences are not a safe moat either: bank charters, exchanges and spectrum have all been opening up.

The zone around the giants really is dead, but "no chance" overstates it. After a giant buys a company, venture funding in its niche falls sharply, and a small company's most common good ending is a sale to a giant. The strongest counterexample is the coding tool Cursor: Microsoft shipped a similar product first, yet four years later Cursor sold to SpaceX for $60 billion. The founders won; the value still ended up inside a giant.

Further out, the shift of income toward capital will probably not reverse, but there are more than two exits. We put the odds that the US labor share fails to regain its 2000 level within twenty years at 70% to 85%: large reversals came almost entirely through war and revolution, and today's antitrust, unions and regulation are weak. Yet every oligarchic stage in history was rewritten within twenty to sixty years by breakup, war or state action. State equity plus handouts worked only where resource rents are large, as in Norway, Alaska and the Gulf. Most cases took a third road: no state equity, but breakups, taxes and labor law. Korea is a fourth: the oligarchs stay put and society slowly stops having children. Severance pay from tech layoffs inflates the high-income brackets too little to change any conclusion.

2. Concentration is real; "final" is not

The US nonfarm labor share index stood at 112.8 in the first quarter of 2000, 96.9 in the fourth quarter of 2022 and 93.4 in the second quarter of 2026 [S1]. Most of the decline predates the mass launch of AI chat products in November 2022, and globalization, platform monopoly and interest rates explain the same reading, so the evidence does not pin it mainly on AI. The bigger problem is "final": no oligarchic stage in history stood still.

The timing does not line up. Korea's top 1% share doubled in 2000-07 as fertility fell from 1.47 to 1.1, but the two moved together (the 1997 financial crisis pushed both); neither led. From 2012 to 2022 the top share fell from 17% to 14% while fertility dropped from 1.3 to 0.78. Across 30 rich countries (our computation from [S3][S4]), the correlation between the latest top 1% share and 2023 fertility is -0.04, and +0.46 without the five East Asian economies. The US is the most unequal, yet its fertility is high for a rich country.

The real confounders are housing and the race for good jobs, not urbanization (81.2% urban in Korea, 80.1% in the US, 2024 [S4]). A median Seoul home cost 16.8 years of household income (2021), Taipei about 15 (2017), Taiwan as a whole 9.3 (2025Q4) [S13]; 46.1% of Korea's KRW 51.7 trillion low-fertility budget in 2022 went to housing [S14]. Korea's non-regular share of workers rose from 32.6% (2003) to a record 38.5% (2026-08) [S12]. Part of this is downstream of concentration, since good jobs cluster in the capital region; women's education and the custom of births only within marriage are not. Fertility reads the cost of a place to start a family; chaebol concentration alone cannot explain Taiwan or Italy.

Korea's rebound is not yet a change of course. Births in 2025 were 254,300, up 6.7% [S5]. First births rose 8.6%, nearly 80% of the increase and 62.4% of all births, while second births rose only 4.4%; the average first-time mother was 33.2 [S6b]. Births rose another 14.5% in January-August 2026, with marriages up 5.2%, the 26th straight monthly rise [S6]. A rebound led by first children fits a catch-up of pandemic-delayed weddings.

Savers outside the groups lost. A typical non-group stock earned about 2.2 points a year less than a deposit over the decade. An equal-weighted basket still won (8.3% a year from 2015), but a few big winners lift that average, and as a survivors-only figure it is an upper bound. Median single stocks lag cash in many markets, since returns are skewed to the right; but the US, measured the same way, shows no slide like Korea's, so Korea's slide is not just method. One tension: the stock readout lit up in 2024, just as births began to rise. We judge the birth rebound a wedding catch-up and the stock readout the true signal.

Of Korea's three traits, the first two hold. A one-engine economy and a broken upward channel both show up in the numbers above. "Cultural zombification" cannot be measured, and the parts that can be measured point the other way: young Koreans vote and protest more than young Americans. Yet street power that twice brought down a president never touched the chaebol: Samsung's Lee Jae-yong was pardoned in August 2022, and his acquittal in the merger case was upheld in July 2025 [S19].

Severance distorts in the right direction, by a negligible amount. An upper bound (our estimate): if all 123,000 people laid off in 2025 were in the US and each got half a year of a $240,000 salary, that is about $14.7 billion, 0.47% of the $200,000-500,000 bracket's income, lifting its share of all income by at most about 0.1 point. If layoffs stop at the end of 2026, data are clean from tax year 2028, published by the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) around 2031. The more useful signal runs the other way: laid-off workers re-employed at lower pay show up in the next year's data.

Irreversibility is a high probability, not a certainty. Our estimate: a great shock in the US within twenty years (major war, revolution, state failure, plague) 10-20%, with a one-half chance that it pulls the labor share back to its 2000 level, giving 5-10%; peaceful means (a tight labor market plus policy) 10-15%. The two add to 15-25%; the inputs are rough, so we widen the reversal range to 15-30%, and "no return to the 2000 level within twenty years" comes out at about 70-85%.

10. Limits

Survivorship. The stock returns include only stocks still trading today; delisted losers drop out, so ordinary stocks are overstated, more so in early windows, which makes the "lags cash" finding conservative. A market-value-weighted version was distorted by share-count changes (7 points a year off the KODEX 200 total return) and dropped. The growth-board index excludes dividends, which we estimate at under 1 point a year; the conclusion stands.

Missing comparisons. Same-method median-stock tests for Taiwan and Japan were not completed because the data source throttled requests; they are the next check on whether Korea's result is just the general weakness of median single stocks.

Not obtained. Korean group market-value shares for 2000-2010 (exchange data need a login); Korean saving rates and mobility between generations; a state-by-state US pairing of fertility and top income shares (we found only the highest top shares, such as New York about 31%, Florida 28.5% and Connecticut 27.3%, 2015 [S59]); like-for-like price-to-income ratios for Seoul and US metros; companies incubated on lab credits; the global size of the customer service, legal, accounting, translation and stock-image markets; approvals of competing surgical robots; and how the labs' required revenue splits among incumbents' revenue, replaced wages and new demand.

What to watch

  1. Whether Korean fertility changes course: a rising share of second births in 2027, with the full-year rate back above 1.0. A rebound in first births alone is still a wedding catch-up.
  2. Whether the ten-year return of Korea's median non-group stock climbs back above cash. If the 2025 Commercial Act revision really changes how profit is shared, it should show up here first.
  3. Whether the US slides down Korea's curve: the median stock's ten-year annualized return falling from 7.8% toward 4% or less, and the share of stocks lagging T-bills rising from 26% toward 40% or more, where Korea stood in 2020-23.
  4. Whether incumbents' revenue follows their share prices: if call-center, stock-image and translation revenue halves as homework help did, "100 to 20" moves from valuation into operations. Alongside it, new bank charter counts and approvals of competing surgical robots.
