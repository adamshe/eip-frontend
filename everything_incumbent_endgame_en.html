Conclusion

Nothing in the world stays scarce. Technology can solve for any material thing and any information. The only true scarcity is man-made scarcity, and man-made scarcity is consensus.

Becoming an incumbent is a process, not a state. By incumbent we mean a company that no longer grows and is priced on its yield, like a landlord collecting rent. Once AI levels people's knowledge and execution, companies turn into incumbents one after another. The process is a slow bleed: hold and wait, and over the long run you lose money.

On the way from scarcity to no scarcity, there are opportunities. Foresight lets you take the high ground early, buy low and sell high. But buying low and selling high has one condition: someone must always come after you to take the baton. That is alpha. Every alpha has an expiry date, and the dates keep getting shorter.

In the end, the one thing that holds while everything else changes is consensus. Consensus is not Bitcoin. Bitcoin is one form consensus takes today, and the one that has best stood the test of time.

We are all living through this, but have not yet noticed. When the big pullback of the next year or two is over and prices come back, people will reflect deeply. The consensus starts forming there and may take three to five years.

1. Society trained people to raise productivity; productivity no longer comes from people

People used to pull apart from one another on two things: what they knew and how well they executed. Learn something more advanced, get things done faster, and you earned more. Society as a whole, from schools to companies, was built to train such people, because productivity came from people.

AI levels both. Knowledge is a question away; execution goes to machines. Once everyone has something, it stops being worth much. When people are leveled, companies that live on people stop differing in how they grow.

This is a huge paradigm shift. We are watching it happen, but few have seen where it ends.

2. Scarcity melts

Scarcity is an advantage one side holds that others cannot get around for now. There are two kinds: informational and physical.

Informational advantages melt first. Courses, code and knowledge are shared for free; even the idea of a good school district will fade.

Physical advantages are melted by information too, only more slowly. Take location. A location is valuable because travel is slow and a person can reach only so much in a day. Once a person can travel three thousand kilometers in half an hour, that plot is one among millions. Behind chip fabs, lithography machines and licenses lies the same thing: others cannot yet build it. AI speeds up exactly the building.

This is not a matter of a year or two; it may take thirty. But the direction does not change: no particular scarcity stays scarce forever.

3. Becoming an incumbent is a slow bleed

A company's excess profit is the stretch of time before its advantage is caught up with. Its share price, though, is set as if it will collect for many years.

When catching up gets faster, holding still means giving back a little of the expected profit every year. You cannot see it at first; the longer you hold, the more you lose. AI speeds up the catching up, so the bleeding speeds up too.

So companies do not become incumbents overnight; it is a process. Today large companies are still priced for a long run of good years, and the bleed hides inside the price. Once people notice, prices reset toward a landlord's rent, and those years bleed fastest.

4. When wages fall short, governments print money to fill the gap, and prices do not rise

Once people are leveled, wages are not enough. Governments will fill the gap, with universal basic income (UBI).

What a government wants is for society to keep turning: some produce, some consume, and people's time has somewhere to go, or there will be unrest. What people actually do does not matter. Money is an agreement everyone accepts; if printed money can buy things, society keeps turning.

Printing money does not push prices up as long as money grows no faster than goods. AI pushes costs down, and goods become more plentiful and cheaper; that cheapness absorbs the new money. Prices hold roughly still while the stock of money keeps growing.

Why the stock of money only grows and never shrinks: three forces lock this in.

  • The private money machine stops. Most of today's money is bank deposits created when banks lend. With no growth, no one borrows to expand, so banks stop creating money.
  • The government machine must take over. Part of the money that fills the wage gap is borrowed and then spent, and that is new money.
  • Not taking over is worse. As goods get cheaper, if money does not grow, debts weigh more in real purchasing power. US federal debt already exceeds a full year of the economy's output; only by issuing more money can it be serviced.

Taxing the owners of AI more and handing the money out could in principle avoid printing. But wealth keeps concentrating in fewer hands, and those hands can move capital away. What cannot be collected must be printed.

Treasury bill yields will keep falling. With no growth, no one borrows to expand; the government is the largest issuer of money and sets the rate, and it will push it toward zero. Saving does not lose, but it does not share in the extra things AI makes.

5. Where the surplus money goes: consensus

Most of the money printed and handed out is spent and absorbed by ever cheaper goods. That is the half that keeps prices still.

The other half is profit the owners cannot spend, which needs a place to be stored. Any asset that can be made more of grows along with money: shares can be issued and new companies listed; houses and land stop being scarce once travel is solved; gold's scarcity is physical and will slowly be melted by mining technology.

Only something whose total is written into a rule that everyone accepts cannot grow. That is consensus.

Consensus is not Bitcoin. Bitcoin is one form consensus takes today, and the one that has best stood the test of time. It holds up on principle: it does one thing, its total is fixed, its rules do not change. Anyone can copy the code; no one accepts the copy.

Chains that try to be everything at once carry more surface to attack, and that is how theft happens. Worse, their rules can be changed: in 2016, after the DAO contract on Ethereum was drained, the community hard-forked and rewrote the ledger. If a few people can change the rules, what is scarce is not consensus but those few people's decisions.

There is only one test for a consensus asset: as time passes, do the rules still stand?

6. Alpha keeps getting shorter; everyday swings shrink, the tails do not

Temporary positions of advantage last shorter, cover less ground and change faster. The faster information spreads, the faster mispricing is erased, and the shorter each window of opportunity becomes.

Everyday swings will keep shrinking. Two things will not go away: news (wars, discoveries, disasters) still arrives, only it is priced in at once and the jumps are steeper; and when everyone uses the same kind of AI and leans the same way, they also sell together when forced to, so crashes may be larger than before.

So what money is left to be made lies not in being faster with information but in two things: bearing the tail risk others will not bear, and holding cash at the moment everyone else is forced to sell.

7. What becomes of nations

States and elites used to need the masses, to work, to fight and to pay taxes. Once productivity no longer comes from people, the rope that tied the two together loosens.

With individual sovereignty rising and AI behind it, each person has great freedom and capability. Capable people may well cluster the way cells do, organizing themselves into a "world zero" that does not live or work alongside everyone else.

There is a counterforce. Power, compute, robots and force are heavy assets; whoever holds them is the state in fact. And a state runs on everyone accepting its money, so capable people leaving and assets that let money leave touch the same nerve.

The tug of war between states and those who leave may well be the main conflict of the next stage. This part cannot yet be seen clearly; it is only a direction.

8. The reasoning stops here

When things truly stop being scarce, the meaning of money itself may change. It may become a kind of ration coupon, a kind of purchasing power, or some other form of distribution. That lies outside today's paradigm, and today's reasoning cannot be carried into it.

If distribution turns into allowances handed out per person that expire, any asset that can be stored loses its use, Bitcoin and cash alike. So this piece reasons only as far as the stretch in which money still means what it means today. We do not bet on the end point.

9. Risks

  • The government itself. A government controls society through everyone accepting its money; an asset that lets people move their money out wholesale runs against that aim. In 1933 the US government ordered private holders to hand in their gold at a fixed price. Whether governments absorb, tax or block Bitcoin is the biggest risk in this chain.
  • Inflation first, during the transition. Jobs disappear first; cheap goods arrive only once compute, power and factories catch up. In the years when support payments come before the goods, prices rise. Printing money offset by AI's cheapness is what comes after the transition.
  • Consensus split. If what people accept shifts to coins created later, Bitcoin becomes just one form among others rather than the form.

10. What to watch

  • After the big pullback of the next year or two, when prices come back, whether public talk starts to ask where growth went.
  • Whether the pay gap between college and high-school graduates keeps narrowing (people being leveled).
  • Whether profit keeps concentrating in a few companies while most companies' margins stay flat or fall (companies becoming incumbents).
  • Whether the growth of the money stock and the growth of prices keep pulling apart (money printed while prices hold).
  • Whether the real yield on Treasury bills moves toward zero.
  • How governments treat Bitcoin: absorb, tax or block.
  • Whether Bitcoin's share of all crypto holds above half (consensus not split).
