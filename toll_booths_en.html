Toll Booths: Many Good Positions, Only Four That Grow on Their Own

We scored 645 large US-listed companies on four plain conditions: neutral, a physical asset money cannot rebuild (including software installed at customers), the whole industry must connect, and each win makes the next one easier. Only TSMC, ASML, Cloudflare and Meta meet all four and also grow fast. Ten more miss one condition but are still growing, and four of them (Arm, Shopify, Axon, DoorDash) clear the growth line. Eight are toll booths that hold firm but no longer grow. The best positions are mostly expensive already; Meta is the exception.

中文版

Conclusion

Of 645 large US-listed companies, only four hold a strong position, keep growing it on their own, and also grow fast: TSMC, ASML, Cloudflare and Meta.

Today, all four positions are tightening. At TSMC, processes of 7 nanometers and below now bring in 79% of wafer revenue, up from 73% six quarters ago. ASML is the only company in the world that can build extreme ultraviolet lithography machines, so an advanced chip plant has no second supplier to buy from. Last October Cloudflare sat in front of 19.8% of all websites; today it is 28.3%. Meta has 3.64 billion people on its apps every day; its strongest challenger, TikTok, took four years to reach a billion people a month, and no funded rival has rebuilt this web of people connected to people in five years. Each win makes the next one easier. Analysts expect all four to grow next year's revenue by more than 20%.

Over the next two or three years, the names to watch are ten companies that miss one leg but are still growing, and four of them clear the growth line. Arm's architecture runs on 350 billion chips, but this year it began selling a chip of its own design and now competes with its licensees. Shopify serves millions of merchants, but merchants have other roads. Axon, which sells body cameras and evidence software to police, and the delivery platform DoorDash lack a physical asset money cannot rebuild, and their claim that the whole industry must connect to them rests for now on old or second-hand share figures. Microsoft, often named as a toll booth, is one condition short: it competes with its own customers.

Eight companies are toll booths: firm positions that no longer grow on their own. Besides the three tower companies and GE Aerospace, counting software installed bases brings in the business-software company SAP, the two chip-design software companies and the life-sciences software company Veeva; they expect revenue growth of only 2% to 14% next year. Visa, Mastercard, the exchanges and the credit bureaus are just as neutral, and their whole industries must connect to them, but their positions are issued cards and records customers keep in their systems, which this note does not count as physical, so they stay one tier down. DISH, the fourth national tower tenant, defaulted in January; that is a wound for the towers, not a steady leak. The only name losing ground is the credit-score company FICO, whose rival product, owned jointly by the three credit bureaus, is being accepted by the mortgage system.

Further out, returns depend on price: the best positions are mostly expensive already, and Meta is the exception. On the last four quarters, Cloudflare's price-to-sales ratio sits at the 80th percentile of its own past five years, TSMC's price-to-earnings ratio at the 77th, and Meta's price-to-earnings ratio at the 27th (14th over ten years). Whether Meta's cheapness is a mispricing depends on the return on its enormous capital spending, and on whether daily-user growth, which slowed from 8% to 3%, stops slowing.

Whole-market counts

The list has 667 lines. Removing 21 duplicate share classes and non-common-stock lines, plus one line that could not be identified, leaves 645 companies. Two groups of scorers used the same rules, and each company was scored once.

  • All four (all three position conditions, and still growing the position): 4, all above the growth line.
  • All three position conditions, no longer growing (toll booths): 8.
  • All three position conditions, but losing ground: 1.
  • One position condition short, still growing: 10, of which 4 clear the growth line; SpaceX's growth figure is questionable.
  • Two position conditions, no longer growing or losing ground: 97. Nine of them clear the growth line (Alphabet, SK hynix, Applied Materials, Lam Research, Seagate, Western Digital, UMC, Sea, Martin Marietta); apart from Alphabet, mostly on the cycle.
  • One condition or none: 525.

The four conditions in plain words

Neutral. It serves every side of its industry and does not compete with its own customers. The test is whether its largest customers also compete with it. TSMC sells no chips under its own brand, so it is neutral. NVIDIA's annual report lists "large cloud services companies with internal teams designing hardware and software" among its competitors, and those companies are its largest customers (two direct customers were 22% and 14% of revenue), so it is not.

A physical asset money cannot rebuild. Factories, machines, networks or permits that a well-funded rival could not rebuild within five years, and also software installed at customers: users, paid seats, installations, devices and chip-design flows, which a funded rival would need more than five years to move. Generic data centers that can be built with borrowed money and rentable computing capacity do not count; nor do apps whose users use several at once and whose rivals built networks of similar size within a few years; nor, in this note, do issued cards, positions and records that customers keep in the company's system, rating opinions or index licences.

The whole industry must connect to it. One side of an industry must connect to it, and this can be counted: customer share, traffic, installed base, transactions. If customers have three or more workable alternatives, it does not count. The scope is stated: worldwide, or one country only.

Each win makes the next one easier. Over the past two years the countable relationship or share has risen, and what was won (data, scale costs, an ecosystem) makes the next win easier. Orders that come with the cycle and growth pushed by sales effort do not count.

Growth is a separate test: analysts' consensus for next-fiscal-year revenue growth of at least 20%.

The reading used here: software installed at customers counts as physical; records customers keep in a company's system, logistics networks and store density do not. If those records also counted, seven companies (Visa, Mastercard, CME Group, Intercontinental Exchange, Equifax, Verisk and Broadridge) would join the toll booths. If logistics networks also counted, Amazon, Walmart and MercadoLibre would become one condition short (none is neutral), and MercadoLibre grows 29.2%.

1. The four that meet all four

TSMC: the strongest position, not expensive on forward earnings, with geography as the price. Its annual report describes it as a "dedicated foundry", one that does not compete with its customers. In the third quarter of 2026, processes of 7 nanometers and below were 79% of wafer revenue (3nm 33%, 5nm 28%, 2nm 8%), compared with 73% in the first quarter of 2025. Consensus for 2027 revenue is up 37.6% in New Taiwan dollars. The three strongest counterexamples: its advanced capacity is almost entirely in Taiwan; Samsung signed a $16.5 billion 2-nanometer contract with Tesla in July 2025, so a second source is growing; and its revenue fell in 2023, because position does not stop the cycle.

ASML: all four, but no bargain. In its annual report's words, "ASML is currently the world's only manufacturer of EUV lithography systems" (EUV is extreme ultraviolet). Every advanced logic and memory plant must connect to its machines, and its October 14 quarterly report says it will ship about 65 of them this year, nearly half again as many as last year. Part of its right to refuse sits with governments: the annual report describes US licence requirements for equipment and services to advanced fabs in China, and Chinese customers were about 29% of its 2025 sales. Three data providers put consensus 2027 revenue growth between 27.7% and 28.7%. "Each win makes the next one easier" is graded questionable: if TSMC does not adopt its newest high-numerical-aperture machines before its next node, that chain weakens.

Cloudflare: a widening position that the market has already paid a high price for. Its network covers 348 cities and connects directly with more than 13,000 networks. In W3Techs' monthly series, the share of all websites using it as a reverse proxy rose from 19.8% on October 1, 2025 to 24.2% on August 1, 2026 and 28.3% on October 30; among sites whose reverse proxy is known, its share rose from 80.9% to 86.1%. October alone added 2 points, much faster than the under 1 point a month before it, so the pace itself needs watching. Consensus for 2027 revenue is up 28.9%. The counterexamples are as clear: Akamai held an edge network for twenty years and became a slow-growing utility; website share is not revenue, and most new sites are free users; and its enterprise value is about 44 times annual revenue. Others have networks too; what is hard to copy is the 28% of websites that sit on Cloudflare's network, and their traffic.

Meta: the cheapest of the four; the people keep coming, more slowly. Daily users went from 3.29 billion in September 2024 to 3.54 billion in September 2025 (+8%) and 3.64 billion in September 2026 (+3%). It does not compete with its advertisers. Advertisers can choose Google, Amazon or TikTok, so "the whole industry must connect" does not hold on the advertiser side; it holds on the people side: more than three billion people are on it every day, and advertisers follow the people. The physical asset is that web of people. The strongest counterexample is TikTok: ByteDance, its owner, does not lack money; TikTok went abroad in 2017 and announced in September 2021 that more than a billion people used it every month, four years on. In that same month Meta had 2.81 billion people every day, so TikTok's monthly count was about a third of Meta's daily one. Meta's daily users have since grown to 3.64 billion: TikTok took a share of attention, not this web. Google spent eight years (2011 to 2019) on a social network and then shut it, another funded attempt that failed. Consensus for 2027 revenue is up 21.6%. Two more counterexamples: from September 2021 to November 2022 Meta lost no users, yet its closing price fell 76.7%; and of about $97.5 billion raised this month, about $60 billion is an at-the-market program that can add shares at any time, so supply needs watching.

2. One short, still growing

Arm: in almost every phone, but now on the field against its customers. Its annual report says that by March 31, 2026 more than 350 billion Arm-based chips had been shipped and more than 22 million developers build on Arm; the same report says that in March 2026 it introduced a chip of its own design, the Arm AGI CPU. From then on it competes with its own licensees, so it is not neutral. Next-fiscal-year consensus growth is 36.4%.

Shopify: a road builder for agent-driven shopping, but merchants have other roads. Its annual report says millions of merchants in more than 175 countries used it at the end of 2025, with $378.4 billion of merchandise volume for the year, up from $292.3 billion. It co-wrote the Universal Commerce Protocol with Google, and its merchants' products appear in ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini. Its installed base counts as physical, but merchants have several workable alternatives, such as Amazon, WooCommerce and Wix, so "the whole industry must connect" does not hold. Next-fiscal-year consensus growth is 26.0%.

Axon: the fastest grower, with an old share figure. It sells body cameras, conducted-energy devices and evidence software to police and prosecutors and does not compete with them. In the second quarter of 2026, annual recurring revenue rose 39% to $1.6 billion, and existing customers spent 126% of what they spent a year earlier. It lacks a physical asset: a funded rival can build the hardware, its annual report gives no count of its evidence-cloud installed base, and Motorola competes with it in every product line. The claim that about 85% of large US city police forces use it dates from 2017, with no recent first-hand figure, so "the whole industry must connect" is questionable.

DoorDash: orders rising, share flat for two years. It cooks nothing; it connects restaurants, couriers and diners. In the second quarter of 2026 organic orders rose 17%, and order value excluding the acquired Deliveroo rose 23%. It lacks a physical asset: diners, restaurants and couriers use several platforms at once, and DoorDash itself went from minority to majority within a few years. Its roughly two-thirds share of US delivery comes from second-hand estimates and has stayed at 66% to 67% for two years, questionable. Its base already includes Deliveroo, so the growth number is not like for like with earlier years.

SpaceX: a real position, a doubtful growth number. In 2025 it flew more than half of the world's launches and over 80% of the mass sent to orbit, 74% of it for its own Starlink. Starlink competes directly with its launch customers (Amazon's Kuiper, SES, Viasat), so it is not neutral. The 268.6% growth consensus comes mainly from the xAI business merged in February 2026 and does not match other forecasts (about $141.6 billion of revenue by 2028).

Intuitive Surgical: three conditions held; the whole industry does not have to connect. At September 30, 2026 it had 12,094 da Vinci systems installed, up 12% in a year, and third-quarter procedures rose 14%. But its customers are hospitals, which can still choose laparoscopic or open surgery, or other robots such as Medtronic's Hugo and Johnson & Johnson's Ottava; robots do roughly 15% to 30% of US general surgery (industry estimates, questionable). If robotic surgery passes half of all surgery, it meets all four.

Microsoft, Apple, Uber, Spotify: one short, and growth below 20%. Microsoft 365 commercial paid seats grew another 6% (fiscal 2026 annual report), so its installed base counts as physical, but the same report says its AI products compete with those of the large cloud providers, "many of which are also current or potential partners", so it is not neutral; growth 19.98%. Apple has 2.35 billion active devices (January 2025), but its App Store competes with developers; growth 10.4%. Uber's roughly 74% share of US ride-hailing is second-hand, and riders and drivers also use rival apps; trips rose 18% in the second quarter of 2026; growth 15.3%. Spotify has 788 million monthly users and 306 million paying subscribers (third quarter of 2026), but switching music apps is easy and its catalogue is licensed from the labels; growth 14.9%.

3. Toll booths that no longer grow

Towers: a wound, not a leak. American Tower's 2025 annual report: DISH failed to meet its payment obligations and as of January 2026 was in default; T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon were 18%, 17% and 14% of its revenue. Crown Castle's annual report: the three big carriers were about 90% of 2025 site rental revenue, and on January 12, 2026 it sent DISH a notice of default and termination. Next-fiscal-year revenue consensus for the three companies rises 3.5%, 1.6% and 2.5%. But excluding DISH and other one-time terminations, Crown Castle's third-quarter organic rent grew 3.5% (4.1% a year earlier) and SBA's about 4%; the net decline covers one year, short of "shrinking for two years". Two things to watch: organic growth in 2027 excluding one-time items, and whether direct-to-phone satellite service starts to take carrier demand away from towers.

GE Aerospace: a firm position, with share gains that came from a rival. Through CFM, its joint venture with Safran, it supplies engines to both Boeing and Airbus and is the sole engine on the 737 MAX; it has about 50,000 commercial and 30,000 military engines in service, and services are about 70% of revenue (annual report). The figure that it holds about 63% of decided A320neo engine choices is second-hand, questionable. Its share gains of the past two years came mainly from a rival's grounded engines, not from its own wheel turning faster. Growth 11.7%.

Software toll booths: SAP, Synopsys, Cadence, Veeva. SAP's annual report runs a dedicated program to move its "installed base customers" from legacy systems to its new suite, and replacing it takes years; Synopsys's and Cadence's annual reports say they build process design kits with the foundries so that chip designs pass manufacturing sign-off, and nine years after Siemens bought Mentor it still ranks third; Veeva serves 1,552 life-sciences customers. All four are neutral and their installed bases do not move, but their industry shares are second-hand figures, graded questionable. Next-fiscal-year consensus growth: 11.9%, 13.2%, 14.4% and 12.3%.

Losing ground: FICO. Its credit score is built into lenders' approval processes, but no first-hand count of that installed base was found, so its physical condition is graded questionable. Its annual report says the three major credit bureaus are both its partners and, through their joint venture VantageScore, sellers of a competing credit score; the mortgage system has begun to accept VantageScore, so its position is being bypassed by a new route (questionable). Growth 6.3%.

4. Why not these

Broadcom: a fine business, two conditions short. The installed base of VMware, which it bought, counts as physical; but its annual report lists "the internal resources of large integrated OEMs" (original equipment manufacturers) among its chip competitors, and its largest customers are designing their own chips, so it is not neutral; its roughly 80% share of data-center switch chips is one investment bank's estimate, with no first-hand data.

Alphabet: a vast installed base, but not neutral and no longer amplifying. Android and Search count as physical; but Search favors its own businesses and its cloud and Gemini compete with its users, so it is not neutral. Growth 26.6%; two position conditions.

CoreWeave: none of the three position conditions. Its largest customer (36% of second-quarter revenue) runs its own cloud business; its data centers are leased and its computing is built with borrowed money, with book debt of about $35.1 billion, above its market value of about $29.8 billion. Its contracted backlog is $103.7 billion and still rising; the problem is not its customer relationships but its balance sheet.

KLA: "near-sole supplier in inspection" does not hold. Its own annual report names Applied Materials, ASML, Hitachi High-Tech, Lasertec and Onto Innovation as competitors. Like Applied Materials and Lam Research, it is a strong supplier with two or three alternatives at each process step, not the one the whole industry must connect to. Next-fiscal-year consensus growth is 18.9%.

Equinix: the position is there, but no evidence its share is rising. It is neutral and its interconnection ecosystem is hard to copy, but customers in each major metro can also use Digital Realty, NTT and others, and no countable share shows the whole industry must connect to it. It added 9,700 and 6,900 net interconnections in the second and third quarters, and revenue consensus rises only 11.3%.

GE Vernova, Walmart, Alibaba: growth below the line. Next-fiscal-year revenue consensus rises 13.9%, 4.3% and 12.6%. GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog plus reservations grew from 116 to 122 gigawatts, but that is an order cycle, not a wheel turning faster.

Mature toll booths: Visa, Mastercard, CME Group, Intercontinental Exchange, Equifax, Verisk, Broadridge. These seven are often called toll booths. All seven are neutral and their whole industries must connect to them, but their positions are issued cards and records customers keep in their systems, not software installed at customers, so this note does not count them as physical; nor are their shares still compounding. The evidence is first-hand: Visa's fiscal 2025 annual report shows nearly 5 billion payment credentials and more than 175 million merchant locations; about 3.39 billion cards carry Mastercard's brands; CME traded a record 28.1 million contracts a day in 2025, with positions and margin held in its own clearing house; ICE's Brent crude contract anchors more than 800 related products, and its MERS database is the system of record for US residential mortgage ownership; Equifax is one of the three reports every mortgage "tri-merge" must include; Verisk's standard policy language covers 32 lines of insurance; Broadridge handles proxy distribution and vote collection for banks and brokers. If records customers keep in a company's system also counted as physical, these seven would join the toll booths, taking them from eight to fifteen. Growth 10.7%, 12.6%, 5.2%, 5.7%, 6.4%, 6.8% and 4.9%.

VeriSign: a rented position. It runs the .com registry, but under a contract with ICANN (the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers) that must be renewed by 2030; like Spotify's catalogue, licensed from the record labels, a rented position is not a toll booth. Growth 10.4%.

Ratings and index companies. S&P Global, Moody's and MSCI are neutral and their whole industries must connect to them, but what they sell is rating opinions and index licences, so this note does not count them as physical; Cboe's exclusive S&P 500 options listing is licensed from S&P, so it is rented. Next-fiscal-year consensus growth is between 3% and 9%.

7. Read inside "everything becomes an incumbent"

This piece scores positions as they stand today. Over a longer span the positions themselves melt: scarcity is an advantage others cannot get around for now, informational advantages are erased first, and physical ones are slowly melted by information too. Behind fabs, lithography machines, networks and software installed at customers lies the same thing, others cannot yet build it, and AI speeds up exactly the building. So "each win makes the next one easier" describes this stretch, not forever.

Read that way, the three tiers each mean something. The eight toll booths are already incumbents: firm positions, no longer growing, priced on their yield, collecting rent while bleeding a little every year. The four still growing on their own are today's foresight: taking the high ground early, on condition that someone comes after you to take the baton, which is why valuation percentiles and growth must be read together; their shelf life will also keep getting shorter, and the checkpoints in section 6 are watching exactly that. The ten that miss one condition but still grow are the next possible opportunities, and the first to be caught up with.

The only scarcity that does not melt is man-made scarcity, which is consensus. That layer is in the companion piece, "The Age When Everything Becomes an Incumbent Is Coming".
