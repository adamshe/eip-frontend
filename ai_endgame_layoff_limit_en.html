AI Endgame: The Limits of Cutting Jobs for Efficiency

By the 2028 election, S&P 500 companies will have about 250,000 fewer US white-collar jobs (3.6%), mostly through roles left unfilled; elections shift announcement dates by weeks, not totals, and the ceiling is near 20%, not half.

Naughty Boss . Empire Integration LLC · Independent research, not investment advice

中文版

A popular projection runs like this: the efficiency multiple that comes from artificial intelligence (AI) agents working around the clock sets the ceiling on how many people companies can cut; companies will rush to cut before policy changes, making 2026 to 2028 an acceleration phase; and they will finish a round of cuts before the presidential election, then pause. This note tests each of the three claims, then works backward along three paths: by the 2028 election and by the end of 2029, how many white-collar jobs will large US companies have shed, and where is the limit?

Conclusion: machines do not lack stamina, they lack the share of work they can deliver; elections move the announcement date, not the total

Between now and the 2028 presidential election, companies in the S&P 500 (the Standard & Poor's 500 index of large US companies) will announce cuts of a little over 300,000 more US white-collar workers. Some will be rehired by other large companies, and some who leave will not be replaced; netting the two, about 250,000 jobs disappear, roughly 3.6% of this group, or about $30 billion a year less in pay. By the end of 2029 the net reduction is about 7%. The cuts will not sprint and then pause to the election calendar; they follow the share of work machines can reliably deliver and the speed at which companies absorb it. At most, the election delays announcements by a few weeks.

Today the total volume of cuts has not accelerated; what has changed is the stated reason. US companies have announced fewer job cuts this year than last, only about a third above the pre-pandemic norm; the change is that AI has become the leading reason given. S&P 500 companies employ about 7 million white-collar workers in the US. By our estimate about 2% of them were named in announced cuts this year, most of it the restructuring that happens every year; at the very largest companies, headcount has barely moved since 2022.

Machines can work without stopping, but how many jobs can go depends mainly on how much of the work they can deliver. When AI agents (AI that calls tools on its own and carries out multi-step work) are tested on real freelance projects, the share they finish to the standard of a human deliverable jumped in one year from 2.5% to about 21%. A job is a bundle of tasks. A machine that runs 24 hours a day in a hundred copies speeds up the part it can do; on testable, readily verified work, trying many times can also widen that part somewhat. But the part that needs human judgment, a signature and accountability cannot be replaced no matter how fast the machine is. So the round-the-clock efficiency multiple mainly sets capacity and speed; the ceiling on cuts is set by the "share of work that can be reliably delivered", about one-fifth today and rising fast.

In the two months before the midterm elections, companies did not rush to cut; they waited, and they put the AI label away. Job cuts announced in August and September were the lowest for those months in three years, and the tracker attributed the wait to energy prices, the war with Iran and possible rate hikes. Cuts filed under AI fell much faster than the total, from about 39,000 in May to about 4,000 in September. New York State added an "is this due to AI?" checkbox to its layoff filing form; in more than a year, over 160 companies filed layoffs and not one ticked it. From New Year's Day, California will require employers to state whether a layoff is a technology replacement. The AI label has started to carry a cost, so companies are more likely to change the wording and the method than to stop.

Most of the acceleration in 2027 and 2028 will happen out of sight. In the main scenario, the annual net loss of white-collar jobs rises step by step from a little over one point to nearly three. Publicly announced cuts will be only somewhat higher than this year; more jobs will simply go unfilled when people leave, as Walmart's head of people put it: revenue keeps growing while headcount stays flat for three years. If agents double in capability every three to four months and companies absorb them faster, the net loss before the election is around 8%; if reliability stalls and early adopters hire people back, it stays under 1%. We give the three scenarios probabilities of 55%, 20% and 25%.

The election will delay announcements by weeks, not halt cuts for years. In three of four presidential election years, cuts announced in October were 24% to 32% lower than in September, while in other years October was almost always higher than September. But full-year totals in election years show no pattern, and there is no make-up wave after the vote. What could really slow the cuts is quality backlash and rehiring in 2027 at customer service and other departments that adopted AI early, machines hitting a reliability wall, or a weakening economy. And the federal bill requiring disclosure of AI layoffs is co-sponsored by legislators from both parties, so that road stays open whoever wins.

Further out, the limit is around one-fifth, not one-half. By 2029, machines may be able to do more than half of white-collar tasks. But the last stretch of every job needs someone to answer for it, demand expands when things get cheaper, and organizations can only be rebuilt so fast. The hardest-hit group, people in their early twenties in the most AI-exposed occupations, fell only about one-fifth in relative terms (about 11% in absolute numbers) over three-plus years, five to six points a year. If all white-collar workers moved at that speed, that would be the ceiling of the accelerated scenario: about 17% fewer by the end of 2029.

For investors, this is a story about distribution, not about index earnings. The roughly $30 billion in pay saved before the election equals only about 1% of a year of S&P 500 net income; money flows from the wages of those laid off to profits and to compute bills. The most certain losers are companies paid by the hour of work or by hiring volume; seat-based software has to wait for the 2027 renewal season. Three readings are worth tracking monthly: the number of cuts attributed to AI, the number of California technology-replacement layoff notices, and large companies' quarterly headcount.

1. Testing the three claims

For each claim we first state the supporting side, then find the three strongest counterexamples, then weigh them. Each claim is half right: AI really is speeding up the replacement of white-collar work; the error lies in attributing the pace to the efficiency multiple and the election calendar.

Claim A, "the efficiency multiple of agents working around the clock sets the ceiling on cuts": partly holds, only on readily verified work. The supporting evidence is hard. METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research, an organization that measures autonomous AI capability) found that the strongest model can complete, at a 50% success rate, tasks that take a human more than 16 hours, and this line has doubled roughly every three months since 2024. The Remote Labor Index tests models on real freelance projects; its pass rate rose in one year from 2.5% to 21%. And on work where checking is cheaper than producing (tested code, account reconciliation, data cleaning), running many parallel attempts nonstop really does raise the deliverable share.

Three counterexamples. Structurally, Amdahl's law says that if the part of a job that can be sped up is p, no matter how fast that part gets, the whole saves at most p; on work that is hard to verify and needs someone accountable, the multiple cannot enlarge p. On reliability, the same models manage only about three-hour tasks at an 80% success rate (secondhand), about four-fifths of freelance projects still fail the standard, and the research firm Gartner predicts that more than 40% of agent projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027. On demand, Salesforce cut customer support from 9,000 people to about 5,000, then this year hired 1,000 new graduates because "demand is greater than ever". Weighing: the multiple raises the ceiling on readily verified tasks, so the claim partly holds; overall, "the reliably deliverable share sets the ceiling and the multiple sets the speed", and that mechanism holds.

Claim B, "if you're going to cut, cut fast; 2026 to 2028 is the acceleration phase": accelerating net white-collar loss partly holds (leaning toward holds); cutting ahead of policy partly holds (leaning toward does not hold). The supporting side: the share of cuts attributed to AI rose from 4.5% last year to 21%; tech-sector cuts in the first nine months already exceed all of last year; Oracle cut 13% in a year and Meta 10%. Policy is moving too: from New Year's Day 2027 California requires disclosure of technology-replacement layoffs, there is a bipartisan federal disclosure bill, and the share of Americans who think AI will reduce jobs rose from 64% to 71%.

Three counterexamples. In the data, total announcements are falling: down 39% year on year in the first nine months, and down 15% even excluding government. AI-attributed cuts were 74,000 in the second quarter and only 18,000 in the third. Structurally, the policies in motion are almost all disclosure, training and longer unemployment benefits, not bans or heavy taxes, and companies answer disclosure by not applying the label: New York's checkbox has gone unticked for more than a year. At the capability gate, cutting people before machines can do the work only hurts the business: Klarna, after shrinking by 40%, reversed course and said it would invest in human customer service; Gartner predicts that half the companies that cut customer service because of AI will rehire by 2027; and by a Financial Times count (secondhand), companies that cited AI when announcing cuts underperformed the Nasdaq by nearly 10% on average over the next 30 trading days. The only legally binding deadline is November-December, before California's disclosure law takes effect, and those data are not out yet, so "cutting ahead of policy" is provisionally judged partly holds, leaning toward does not hold.

Claim C, "finish a round of cuts before the election, then pause": delaying by a few weeks before the vote partly holds (leaning toward holds); pausing after a round is completed does not hold. The supporting side: before the 2012 election, Lockheed Martin planned to send advance layoff notices to all employees four days before the vote, and withdrew the plan on October 1 after the White House and the Labor Department stepped in. Twenty days after the 2018 midterms, General Motors announced 14,000 cuts and the president attacked it at once. Of four presidential election years, in three (2016, 2020, 2024) October was 24% to 32% below September, and in both 2016 and 2024 Challenger wrote that companies seemed to be "waiting for the election results". In seven of eight non-presidential years, October was at least 12% above September.

Three counterexamples. On full-year totals, the election years 2012, 2016 and 2024 show no consistent direction against the years before and after. Month by month, in 2012 announcements in the month before the vote were 41% above September, and in 2016 the November after the election was the low of the year, with no post-election make-up. Structurally, political pressure reaches companies with big federal contracts (Lockheed) or companies the president names (General Motors); on 500 companies as a whole it is a weak force. Their real calendar is the budget year and earnings season. 2028 is new ground, the first time AI layoffs are an election issue, so the historical base rate is only a weak test.

The live test from the midterms is weak: historically October has always been above September in midterm years, so the low in August and September cannot be read as "holding back before the vote". Challenger's September report attributed the wait to energy prices, the war with Iran, possible rate hikes and health-care costs, and did not mention the election. The AI share fell from over 30% in June and July to 7% and 9%, faster than the total, which looks more like dropping the label. But the label has not vanished: on October 23 American Express cut about 3,500 people, and its chief financial officer (CFO) called it "preparing for AI".

2. Back-calculation: three paths, three scenarios

The base. Of the S&P 500, 496 companies report full-time headcount, totaling 28.78 million people. Using the occupational structure from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) for May 2025, and counting management, business and financial, computer, engineering, science, legal, design and media, office and administrative, and non-retail sales representatives as white-collar, the white-collar share by industry ranges from 8% in food service and accommodation to 97% in finance and insurance. Applied to the S&P 500's industry mix, white-collar workers number about 11.95 million (range 10.5 million to 13.4 million), of whom about 7 million are in the US (overseas white-collar staff are concentrated at outsourcers such as Accenture, Cognizant and IBM). The layoff report counts cuts announced by US employers, so US white-collar workers are the denominator, with pay valued at $115,000 per person a year (the US white-collar average is $94,000; large companies pay more).

Path A, extrapolating from the announced pace. Nationwide announced cuts excluding government were about 720,000 in 2024, about 900,000 in 2025, and about 730,000 for 2026 annualized from the first nine months, against a 2015-2019 norm of 535,000. Assuming S&P 500 white-collar workers account for one-fifth of these announcements (22% in 2026, when tech's share is high), 2024 to 2026 come to about 145,000, 180,000 and 160,000, against a norm of about 110,000 a year on the same basis. Treating the excess over the norm (about 54,000 in 2026) as structural reduction that is not replaced, the main scenario multiplies it by 1.3 each year, the accelerated by 2 and the stalled by 0.8. This path counts only what is announced, not what quietly goes unfilled, so it is the low end.

Path B, working back from machine capability. Net reduction = reliably deliverable share of tasks x companies' absorption rate, minus what has already happened. The share is 21% today; in the main scenario it rises to 45%, 55% and 65% by end-2027, October 2028 and end-2029; accelerated 60%, 72%, 80%; stalled 28%, 32%, 35%. The absorption rate is the fraction of "what machines can do" that actually turns into fewer people. Net reduction so far is at most about 0.8% (13 large companies with annual reports went from 6.34 million people in 2022 to 6.21 million in 2025, down only 2%, and up 3% excluding Walmart and Intel; converting both by white-collar share and taking the upper end gives about 0.8%); divided by one-fifth capability, that gives a starting rate of about 4%. It doubles every 18 months in the main scenario, every 12 months accelerated and every 30 months stalled, capped at 40%.

Path C, working back from company finances. If revenue grows 5% to 8% a year (current S&P 500 members grew 4.1%, 5.5% and 7.8% in 2023 to 2025) and white-collar output per person rises 7% to 10% a year, white-collar headcount falls by about 2% net a year. That is the same order as a JPMorgan Chase executive's "conservative" estimate of 10% fewer operations staff and Walmart's "revenue keeps growing, headcount flat for three years", so it serves as the main scenario. Accelerated takes a 5% net reduction a year (Amazon cut about 9% of corporate staff in one quarter, Meta 10%, Oracle 13%); stalled takes no change. This path is sensitive to the revenue cycle: third-quarter revenue grew 12.7%, and by its logic the net reduction right now is about zero.

3. Pause points and the limit

First, what each side wants. Companies most want to protect margins during the years when depreciation surges, and next not to become the next General Motors named by the president; together the best move is to cut quietly. Both parties most want to be seen protecting jobs before the vote, and next not to hinder the AI industry: the current administration uses an executive order to challenge state AI laws; Democrats propose disclosure, training funds and longer unemployment benefits; and the bill co-sponsored by Republican Hawley and Democrat Warner is also about disclosure. The two sides' best moves meet at one point: legislation requires disclosure, and companies switch to not filling vacancies and to voluntary departures. Microsoft's first-ever voluntary buyout program this year, covering about 7% of its US employees, has exactly this shape. No side's best strategy includes "rush cuts before the vote, rest after it".

What readings would change our verdict: if September and October 2028 together are at least 20% below the same months of 2027, while November 2028 to February 2029 together are at least 20% higher, "delay before the vote" is upgraded to holds; if both stretches match the prior year, it is downgraded to does not hold. This year's rehearsal comes out in early November and early December: if November and December announcements are clearly above the same months of 2025, the two explanations must be separated by region and function. Concentration in California and in technical roles points to "getting ahead of California's disclosure law"; a nationwide spread points to "post-election make-up".

The limit: machines can do more than half, but actual reductions top out around one-fifth. Extrapolating capability, by 2029 agents may reliably deliver 50% to 80% of white-collar tasks; in Anthropic's user survey, more than a third of respondents expect AI to be able to do most of their work next year. Actual reductions will stop far below that, for four reasons: the last stretch of a job needs someone to judge, sign and carry legal responsibility; the more machines do, the more the people who check them matter; demand expands when things get cheaper; and organizations can only be rebuilt so fast. A single company can move fast: Intel shrank by more than 30% in three years, Oracle by 13% in one. But at the group level, the young people in their early twenties in the most AI-exposed occupations tracked by Stanford fell about 19% in relative terms (about 11% in absolute numbers) over three-plus years, five to six points a year, the fastest speed seen so far. If all white-collar workers moved at that speed, that would be the accelerated scenario's ceiling, about 17% fewer by the end of 2029. So a reasonable upper bound is around one-fifth, with the main scenario around 7%, and not one-half.

4. What this means for investors

The money comes out of the laid-off workers' wages and flows to profits and compute bills, and somewhat later to customers. In the main scenario, the roughly $30 billion in pay saved before the election equals only 1% of the S&P 500's roughly $2.9 trillion a year in net income, and about 4% of the 2026 capital spending of the five companies Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta and Oracle (about $800 billion combined). Meta's 10% cut saves about $3.1 billion a year, 2% of its capital spending this year. "Freeing up money for AI" frees up little on the books; what it protects is the look of margins while depreciation surges. At the index level this is not an earnings story but a continuation of the step down in labor's share of income: the labor share of the US nonfarm business sector fell to 52.8% in Q2 2026, the lowest since records began in 1947 (BLS Productivity and Costs, FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) series PRS85006173, https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PRS85006173 ). For now the savings stay with employers; once competitors all rent the same agents, 2027 to 2029 will hand them to customers through competition.

The certain losers are companies whose revenue is tied to white-collar headcount or hiring volume. Outsourcers that bill by the hour, staffing agencies and recruiting platforms earn revenue directly in step with new white-collar jobs, and the main scenario is itself "revenue up, headcount flat", so this group has no mechanism for a reversal. Seat-based software is still undecided: fewer white-collar workers at customers means fewer seats, but through this year this group's revenue and margins have both risen; the test comes in the 2027 renewal season. Office buildings and consumer spending aimed at highly paid white-collar workers are a slower layer.

The most certain beneficiaries are not the companies that cut, but the chokepoints that collect payment. Most of what cutting companies save goes to compute, power and chips; whether their own margins hold depends on whether they have something rivals cannot rent. Banks, insurers and payment networks, with high white-collar cost shares and pricing power, will see the benefit first, but insurance employment is already falling this year, so for this layer the benefit is already happening and the market has mostly seen it.

Five leading indicators. Monthly: the number of AI-attributed cuts in the layoff report; if it climbs back above 20,000 a month in November and December, the election only shifted timing. The number of California technology-replacement layoff notices from 2027, the first count with legal consequences. The share of computer-occupation workers unemployed for six months or more. Quarterly: total headcount in the quarterly reports (Form 10-Q, filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission) of the largest tech companies; and in 2027, net revenue retention at seat-based software companies.

The first quarter of 2027 is when the three sets of numbers appear together for the first time. If accelerating net white-collar reduction holds, the acceleration will be concentrated in the January-February 2027 budget season. California's disclosure law will have just taken effect and seat-based software will be entering renewal season, so the market will see layoffs, margins and renewals together for the first time. Before then, companies that sell hours have a clear direction but are already priced for it, and seat-based software is not yet certain enough.

In one sentence: white-collar reductions will probably accelerate in 2027 and 2028 (the main and accelerated scenarios together carry 75%), mainly by not replacing people who leave; the election moves announcements by a few weeks at most and does not change the total.

6. Unverified points and open questions

  • The number of S&P 500 white-collar workers is an estimate built from industry occupational structure, not company disclosure. The roughly 58% in the US, pay of $115,000 per person, and S&P 500 white-collar workers' one-fifth share of nationwide non-government announcements (a bottom-up sum of known large-company announcements in the same years runs about 12%-20%) are all estimates; an error of 20% in any one of them shifts Table 3 by the same proportion but does not change the order of the three scenarios.
  • The softest input is the absorption rate: headcount at the 13 large companies was essentially flat from 2022 to 2025, so the 4% starting rate may be high (strictly, it may be close to zero). Path A treats "announcements above the norm" as structural reduction while not counting quiet non-replacement, a bias of another kind. The main scenario embeds a prior that capability keeps rising fast; the 25% stalled scenario is there for that.
  • Path C is sensitive to the revenue cycle: with current-quarter revenue up 12.7%, by its logic the net reduction right now is about zero, and the main scenario's -2% a year requires output per person to grow clearly faster than revenue.
  • AI-attributed cuts are self-reported by companies. Citing AI was once treated as good news (Oxford Economics' "dressing up" view), but in 2026 companies that cited AI underperformed the Nasdaq (secondhand), and New York filings had zero ticks, which shows that the label's cost is changing; AI-attributed counts will increasingly understate real replacement.
  • METR's 16 hours is already at the limit of measurement, and the three hours at an 80% success rate is secondhand; the Remote Labor Index leaderboard has no dates or confidence intervals. The 2027-2029 shares on the capability path are extrapolations, not measurements.
  • The October 2026 layoff report comes out after the midterm elections, so this note's midterm test uses data only through September; the November and December readings are the real answer to that test.
  • Large-company headcounts are taken at end-2025 or the latest fiscal year-end; quarterly headcount at the end of September 2026 (after Meta's cuts) was not checked. The basis of Microsoft's roughly $175 billion capital spending (calendar or fiscal year, with or without finance leases) is not reconciled; the five-company total of about $800 billion adds different fiscal-year bases directly.
  • The September-October cuts at American Express, FICO, Workday and others come from media and layoff-tracking sites, some from a single source.
  • Historically, after automated teller machines (ATMs) spread, the number of bank tellers rose rather than fell for years, and after spreadsheets bookkeepers declined while accountants grew. Such precedents point the same way as this note's conclusion, but their figures were not verified in this round and are not part of the argument.
  • For November 2016 announcements, the Challenger page headline says 23,936, while the body and several secondary sources say 26,936; the table uses the latter.
